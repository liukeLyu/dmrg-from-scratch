This course builds DMRG, the standard method for finding ground states of one-dimensional quantum chains, from scratch. Our test case throughout is the transverse-field Ising chain (TFIM),

\(H = -\sum_i X_iX_{i+1} - h\sum_i Z_i\)

Neighbouring spins want to line up along \(x\); the field \(h\) pushes each spin along \(z\). For \(h < 1\) the spins order along \(x\), for \(h > 1\) they follow the field, and \(h = 1\) is the critical point between the two. The model is a good test because it can be solved exactly, so every DMRG number can be checked.

Why do we need a special method at all? A quantum state of \(L\) spins-1/2 is a list of \(2^L\) complex amplitudes \(\psi_{s_1 s_2\cdots s_L}\). Each new spin doubles the list.

\(L = 50 \;\Rightarrow\; 2^{50} \approx 10^{15}\) amplitudes \(\approx 18\) petabytes

Exact diagonalization, which stores this whole list and finds the lowest eigenvector of \(H\), stops around \(L \approx 40\) no matter how big the computer. To go further we need a way to store only the part of \(\psi\) that matters.

DMRG's bet is that ground states are special. When \(H\) only couples nearby spins, as the TFIM does, almost all of those \(2^L\) numbers are redundant. The next four lessons show that entanglement measures how much redundancy there is.

How much memory does a complex state vector of 30 spins need, in double precision?

\(2^{30} \approx 1.07\times10^9\) amplitudes × 16 bytes ≈ 17 GB. Doable on a big node, but 40 spins would need 17 TB.

To compress a state we first need to know how much the two halves of the chain share. Cut the chain into a left part and a right part. Reshape \(\psi\) into a matrix: rows label the left spins, columns the right spins. Its singular value decomposition is

\(|\psi\rangle = \sum_a S_a\, |a\rangle_L\, |a\rangle_R, \qquad S_1 \ge S_2 \ge \dots \ge 0,\;\; \sum_a S_a^2 = 1\)

The \(|a\rangle_L\) and \(|a\rangle_R\) are orthonormal states of each half. The singular values \(S_a\) are the Schmidt values. They tell you everything about the entanglement across that cut.

One nonzero \(S_a\) means a product state: no entanglement. Many comparable \(S_a\) means strong entanglement.

In the code: np.linalg.svd(psi.reshape(2**l, -1)), used inside dmrg.mps.from_dense.

What are the Schmidt values of the Bell pair \((|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt2\)?

Two equal values, \(S_1 = S_2 = 1/\sqrt2\). The matrix is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\mathbb{1}\).

Square the Schmidt values to get probabilities \(p_a = S_a^2\). The entanglement entropy of the cut is their Shannon entropy:

\(S = -\sum_a p_a \log_2 p_a\)

We use base 2, so entropy is measured in bits: a Bell pair carries exactly 1 bit of entanglement.

It is the von Neumann entropy of either half's reduced density matrix, whose eigenvalues are exactly the \(p_a\). If only \(\chi\) Schmidt values are nonzero, \(S \le \log_2 \chi\). Turn that around: a state with entropy \(S\) needs at least about \(2^{S}\) Schmidt values to be described well.

In the code: dmrg.mps.entropy(S).

The GHZ state \((|00\cdots0\rangle + |11\cdots1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) of 10 spins. What is the entropy of any cut?

1 bit for every cut, because there are always exactly two Schmidt values \(1/\sqrt2\). Lots of spins, very little entanglement per cut.

A random state has entropy close to the maximum, \(L/2\) bits, at the middle cut. That is the volume law, and it is hopeless to compress.

Ground states are different. Take a 1D Hamiltonian that only couples nearby spins and has a gap, a finite energy cost to the first excited state (the TFIM away from \(h = 1\)). Its ground-state entropy stays bounded as \(L\) grows: only spins near the cut are entangled across it. This is the 1D area law (Hastings, arXiv:0705.2024).

At a critical point, where the gap closes (the TFIM at \(h = 1\)), the entropy does grow, but only logarithmically. For a block of \(l\) sites in an infinite chain, \(S = \tfrac{c}{3}\log_2 l\). The number \(c\), the central charge, labels the kind of critical point; for the Ising transition \(c = 1/2\), and the last lessons measure it with DMRG.

Your dataOpen TFIM chain, \(L = 100\): the middle-cut entropy is about 0.84 bits at \(h = 1\), 0.22 bits at \(h = 1.5\), and almost 0 at \(h = 0.5\). Notebook 3.

Why does the area law make ground states compressible?

Bounded entropy means the Schmidt values decay fast, so a fixed number \(\chi\) of them captures the state at every cut, independent of \(L\).

Here is how the area law turns into savings. If the Schmidt values decay fast, throw away the small ones. Keep the largest \(\chi\) and renormalize. The squared error of the state is the weight you discarded:

\(\varepsilon = \sum_{a > \chi} S_a^2\)

This is the best possible rank-\(\chi\) approximation of that cut (the Eckart–Young theorem). \(\chi\) is called the bond dimension, and it is the single knob that controls DMRG's accuracy and cost.

Your dataGround state of 12 spins at \(h = 1\), full \(\chi = 64\). Keeping \(\chi = 4\) gives infidelity \(6\times10^{-6}\). Keeping \(\chi = 8\) gives \(9\times10^{-10}\). Notebook 1.

The Schmidt values squared are 0.9, 0.09, 0.009, 0.001. What is the error if you keep \(\chi = 2\)?

\(\varepsilon = 0.009 + 0.001 = 0.01\), so fidelity about 0.99.

From here on we draw tensors as pictures. There are only two rules. A shape is a tensor, a block of numbers. Each line sticking out of it is one index: a vector has one leg, a matrix two, a 3-index tensor three.

Joining two legs means summing over that index. Two matrices joined by a leg are their matrix product, \((AB)_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}\):

The open legs that remain tell you what the result is. A state of three spins is one tensor with three open legs, one per spin:

Later lessons give shapes extra meaning: diamonds hold Schmidt values, triangles are tensors in canonical form, squares are pieces of the Hamiltonian, and a thick line is several indices grouped into one.

What do you get if you join the two legs of a matrix to each other?

Its trace, \(\sum_i M_{ii}\). No open legs are left, so the result is a single number.

The last unit said ground states can be compressed. This unit shows the format they are compressed into, the matrix product state (MPS), by building one by hand for a real ground state: three spins of the TFIM at the critical point \(h = 1\).

For this hand example only, we turn the axes so the coupling is along \(z\): \(H = -\sum_i Z_iZ_{i+1} - h\sum_i X_i\). It is the same physics, but now the two ordered patterns are the familiar 000 and 111. The code uses the form from the first lesson.

A state of three spins is 8 numbers \(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3}\), one for each pattern. Rounded to three digits, the ground state is:

\(\begin{array}{cccccccc} 000 & 001 & 010 & 011 & 100 & 101 & 110 & 111 \\ 0.532 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.532 \end{array}\)

Our goal over the next few lessons: rewrite these 8 numbers as three small pieces, one per spin, that multiply together to give back any \(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3}\) we ask for. Those pieces are the MPS.

Why is \(\psi_{000} = \psi_{111}\), and \(\psi_{001} = \psi_{110}\)?

Flipping every spin, \(\prod_i X_i\), commutes with \(H\), and the ground state is symmetric under it. So each pattern and its flipped partner have the same amplitude.

Arrange the 8 numbers in a grid. Let the row be the first spin \(s_1\) (2 rows) and the column be the other two spins \(s_2 s_3\) (4 columns: 00, 01, 10, 11):

\(M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.532 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 \\ 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.532 \end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix} \leftarrow s_1 = 0 \\ \leftarrow s_1 = 1 \end{matrix}\)

Nothing was computed. These are the same 8 numbers, only laid out differently. In NumPy it is psi.reshape(2, 4).

Why bother? This matrix is exactly the cut between spin 1 and the rest. Its singular values are the Schmidt values of that cut (see "The Schmidt decomposition"), so the next step will tell us how strongly spin 1 is entangled with spins 2 and 3.

Which entry of \(M\) holds \(\psi_{110}\)?

Row \(s_1 = 1\), column \(s_2 s_3 = 10\): bottom row, third entry, 0.296.

Take the SVD, \(M = U\,S\,V^\dagger\). A computer gives

\(U = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0.967 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.254 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(V^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.605 & 0.366 & 0.366 & 0.605 \\ 0.660 & 0.254 & -0.254 & -0.660 \end{pmatrix}\)

Multiplying \(S\) into \(V^\dagger\) scales its rows by the Schmidt values:

\(SV^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.585 & 0.354 & 0.354 & 0.585 \\ 0.168 & 0.064 & -0.064 & -0.168 \end{pmatrix}\)

Look at what each factor depends on. \(U\) has rows labelled by \(s_1\) only. \(SV^\dagger\) has columns labelled by \(s_2 s_3\) only. They are glued by a new, summed index \(a\), the column of \(U\) and the row of \(SV^\dagger\):

\(\psi_{s_1, s_2 s_3} = \sum_{a=1}^{2} U_{s_1 a}\,\big(SV^\dagger\big)_{a,\, s_2 s_3}\)

Keep \(U\) as the tensor of spin 1. The index \(a\) is a bond: the only channel through which spin 1 talks to the rest. The Schmidt values 0.967 and 0.254 say spin 1 is entangled, but not much: 0.34 bits.

If we kept only the largest Schmidt value, what squared error would this cut make?

The discarded weight \(0.254^2 \approx 0.065\) (see "Truncation"). The bond would shrink from 2 to 1.

What is left is \(R = SV^\dagger\), with rows \(a\) and columns \(s_2 s_3\). Do the same trick again, but now let the row be the pair \((a, s_2)\) and the column be \(s_3\):

\(R' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.585 & 0.354 \\ 0.354 & 0.585 \\ 0.168 & 0.064 \\ -0.064 & -0.168 \end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix} \leftarrow a{=}1,\, s_2{=}0 \\ \leftarrow a{=}1,\, s_2{=}1 \\ \leftarrow a{=}2,\, s_2{=}0 \\ \leftarrow a{=}2,\, s_2{=}1 \end{matrix}\)

Again the same 8 numbers, rearranged. SVD again, \(R' = U' S' V'^\dagger\):

\(U' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & 0.646 \\ 0.686 & -0.646 \\ 0.170 & 0.288 \\ -0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.967 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.254 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(V'^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S'V'^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 & 0.684 \\ 0.179 & -0.179 \end{pmatrix}\)

A new bond \(b\) of size 2 appears between spins 2 and 3. Keep \(U'\) as the tensor of spin 2. It has three indices: the old bond \(a\) on its left, its own spin \(s_2\), and the new bond \(b\) on its right. The leftover \(S'V'^\dagger\) is the tensor of spin 3, with indices \(b\) and \(s_3\).

In the code this loop is dmrg.from_dense: reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), carry \(SV^\dagger\) on.

The new Schmidt values \(S'\) are again 0.967 and 0.254. Why the same as at the first cut?

The cut between spins 2 and 3 is the mirror image of the cut between spins 1 and 2, and the open 3-site chain is symmetric under reflection.

We now have one piece per spin. Fixing the spin value turns each piece into a small matrix. Rows of \(U\) give spin 1; the rows of \(U'\) with \(s_2 = 0\) or \(1\) give spin 2; the columns of \(S'V'^\dagger\) give spin 3:

\(A_1^{0} = (0.707\;\;0.707),\qquad A_1^{1} = (0.707\;\;{-0.707})\)
\(A_2^{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & 0.646 \\ 0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix},\qquad A_2^{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & -0.646 \\ -0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(A_3^{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 \\ 0.179 \end{pmatrix},\qquad A_3^{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 \\ -0.179 \end{pmatrix}\)

To get any amplitude, pick the matrix matching each spin and multiply them in order:

\(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3} = A_1^{s_1}\,A_2^{s_2}\,A_3^{s_3}\)

For \(\psi_{000}\): \((0.707\;\;0.707)\,A_2^0 = (0.605\;\;0.660)\), then \(0.605\times0.684 + 0.660\times0.179 = 0.532\). It matches the table. A row vector, times a matrix, times a column vector gives a number. That is why this is called a matrix product state.

Compute \(\psi_{010}\) the same way.

\((0.707\;\;0.707)\,A_2^{1} = (0.365\;\;{-0.253})\), then \(0.365\times0.684 - 0.253\times0.179 \approx 0.204\). It matches.

For \(L\) spins, repeat step 3 until one spin is left: reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), carry \(SV^\dagger\) to the right. You always end with

\(\psi_{s_1 s_2 \cdots s_L} = A_1^{s_1} A_2^{s_2} \cdots A_L^{s_L}\)

In code each site is one 3-index array A[left, phys, right]: left bond, spin, right bond. The first site's left bond and the last site's right bond have size 1, which is why the product comes out as a number.

Notice that our 3-spin MPS holds \(4 + 8 + 4 = 16\) numbers (\(A_1\) is \(2\times2\), \(A_2\) is \(2\times2\times2\), \(A_3\) is \(2\times2\)), twice the 8 we started with. Compression only appears for long chains, where two facts take over:

  • Done exactly, the bonds grow up to \(2^{L/2}\) in the middle.
  • At each SVD we can keep only the largest \(\chi\) Schmidt values, as in "Truncation". For ground states the discarded weight is tiny even at modest \(\chi\), so every bond stays small while \(L\) grows.

In the code: dmrg.from_dense(psi, L), and back with dmrg.to_dense(mps).

What are the bond sizes of an exact MPS for a random 8-spin state?

1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Each bond is limited by the smaller side of its cut, \(\min(2^l, 2^{L-l})\). A random state is volume-law entangled, so truncating it would lose a lot.

An MPS has \(L\) tensors of size at most \(\chi \times d \times \chi\), with \(d = 2\) for spins. So the storage is about

\(L\, d\, \chi^2 \quad\text{instead of}\quad d^{L}\)

Linear in \(L\) instead of exponential. The price is that not every state fits: only states whose entanglement is at most about \(\log_2\chi\) bits at every cut. By the area law, ground states do.

How many numbers does an MPS with \(L = 100\), \(d = 2\), \(\chi = 64\) hold?

\(100 \times 2 \times 64^2 \approx 8\times10^5\). Under 7 MB. The full vector would have \(2^{100} \approx 10^{30}\) entries.

We found one MPS for our state, but it is not the only one. Take any invertible matrix \(X\) and insert \(X X^{-1} = \mathbb{1}\) on a bond:

\(A_i \to A_i X, \qquad A_{i+1} \to X^{-1} A_{i+1}\)

The state does not change, but the tensors do. So the MPS of a state is far from unique. This looks like a nuisance. It is actually a tool: we can choose \(X\) to make the tensors as convenient as possible. Those convenient choices are the canonical forms, next.

If you multiply \(A_3\) by 2 and \(A_7\) by 1/2, does the state change?

No. Scalars commute through the product, so the factors cancel. It is the simplest gauge transformation.

Go back to the \(U\) you kept in Step 2:

\(U = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}\)

Its two columns are orthonormal. Each has length 1, \(0.707^2 + 0.707^2 = 1\), and they are perpendicular, \(0.707\times0.707 + 0.707\times(-0.707) = 0\). In matrix language, \(U^\dagger U = \mathbb{1}\).

Now draw it. Put \(U\) on top of its complex conjugate \(U^*\) and join their spin legs. Joining \(s_1\) sums over it, which is exactly \(U^\dagger U\). So the pair is the identity, a plain line from \(a\) to \(a'\):

We draw such tensors as triangles pointing right. A triangle joined to its mirror image collapses into a line.

This is not luck. Every \(U\) from an SVD has orthonormal columns, so every tensor the recipe kept, \(U\) and \(U'\), is a triangle. They are called left-canonical.

Is \(U'\) from Step 3 a triangle too? Its first column is \((0.686,\ 0.686,\ 0.170,\ -0.170)\). Square these four entries and add them: do you get 1?

Yes: \(0.686^2 + 0.686^2 + 0.170^2 + 0.170^2 = 0.471 + 0.471 + 0.029 + 0.029 = 1.00\). The second column, \((0.646,\ -0.646,\ 0.288,\ 0.288)\), also gives 1, and multiplying the two columns entry by entry and adding gives 0. So \(U'^\dagger U' = \mathbb{1}\). For \(U'\) the joined legs are its left bond \(a\) and its spin \(s_2\); only \(b\) stays open.

Measure something. How much does spin 3 point along \(x\)? That is \(\langle\psi|X_3|\psi\rangle\). As a diagram: the MPS on top, its conjugate below, all spin legs joined, with the operator \(X\) inserted on spin 3:

Start from the left. \(A_1\) and \(A_1^*\) are a triangle pair joined on their spin leg, so they collapse into a line:

Now \(A_2\) and \(A_2^*\) are joined on their left (by that line) and on their spin leg. Same pattern, so they collapse too:

Only spin 3's own tensor is left. With \(A_3^{0} = (0.684,\ 0.179)\) and \(A_3^{1} = (0.684,\ -0.179)\), and \(X\) swapping \(0 \leftrightarrow 1\):

\(\langle X_3\rangle = 2\,A_3^{0}\cdot A_3^{1} = 2\,(0.684^2 - 0.179^2) = 0.872\)

The exact value is 0.871; the gap is rounding. The first two spins never entered the arithmetic. This is why DMRG keeps its tensors canonical: anything measured at the non-triangle site only needs that site, because the rest of the chain cancels.

What do you get if you leave out \(X\) in the last picture?

The norm \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\): the sum of the squared entries of \(A_3\), \(0.684^2 + 0.684^2 + 0.179^2 + 0.179^2 = 1.00\).

Run the recipe from the right end instead: split off spin 3 first, keep \(V^\dagger\) as its tensor, and carry \(US\) to the left. Each kept \(V^\dagger\) has orthonormal rows, \(V^\dagger V = \mathbb{1}\).

We draw it as a triangle pointing left and call it \(B\). It cancels with its conjugate when they are joined from the right:

So the rule for both kinds is the same: a triangle cancels when you join it to its mirror image on every leg except the one it points toward.

You never need the full state to get canonical tensors. Sweep along any MPS and re-split each tensor with an SVD (or a cheaper QR decomposition), pushing the leftover into the next site.

In the code: dmrg.left_canonicalize and dmrg.right_canonicalize.

A right-canonical \(B\) has legs \(a\) (left), \(s\) (spin), \(b\) (right). Which legs do you join with \(B^*\) to make it cancel?

\(s\) and \(b\). The left leg \(a\), the side the triangle points to, stays open, and the pair becomes a line from \(a\) to \(a'\).

Make everything left of a bond left-canonical and everything right of it right-canonical:

\(|\psi\rangle = A_1 \cdots A_i \; C \; B_{i+1} \cdots B_L\)

Our 3-spin example is already in this form: \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are triangles and \(A_3 = S'V'^\dagger\) is the centre \(C\).

All the information about the state's norm and entanglement now sits in the small matrix \(C\) at the centre. Because both blocks are orthonormal bases, \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum |C_{ab}|^2\), and the singular values of \(C\) are the Schmidt values of that cut.

This is the form DMRG works in. It moves the centre site by site and always optimizes at the centre.

Why is it cheap to read off entanglement in this form?

You only need the SVD of the \(\chi\times\chi\) matrix \(C\), not anything involving the rest of the chain.

So far we have built an MPS from a full state vector. In DMRG we will never have that vector, so every quantity has to be computed from the tensors alone. The simplest one is the overlap \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) of two MPS. As a diagram it is a ladder: the tensors \(A\) of \(\psi\) on top, the conjugated tensors \(B^*\) of \(\phi\) below, spin legs joined:

Contracting everything at once would rebuild the \(2^L\) vector. Instead, zip the ladder up from the left, one rung at a time. Keep a small matrix \(E\) that holds everything contracted so far (the dashed box). It has two open legs: the bond sticking out of the top row, and the one sticking out of the bottom row.

Where does \(E\) start? The first tensors \(A_1\) and \(B_1\) have a left bond of size 1 (a leg with only one value, drawn or not). Before anything is contracted, \(E\) is the \(1\times1\) matrix \((1)\): it multiplies by one and changes nothing. With that start, the first rung uses the same step as every other rung, so no special case is needed for the edge.

One step of the zipper joins \(E\) to the next rung and gives a new \(E\):

In formulas, summing over the three joined legs \(x\), \(y\) and \(s\):

\(E^\text{new}_{ab} = \sum_{x,\,y,\,s} E_{xy}\; A^{s}_{xa}\; \overline{B^{s}_{yb}}\)

Each step costs about \(d\chi^3\), so the whole overlap costs \(L d \chi^3\) instead of \(2^L\). At the right end both open bonds have size 1, and \(E\) is a single number: the overlap. Energies work the same way, with the Hamiltonian in the middle of the ladder; the next unit writes the Hamiltonian in a form that fits there.

In the code: dmrg.overlap.

What size is \(E\) in the middle of the chain, if every bond has dimension \(\chi\)?

\(\chi \times \chi\): one leg for the top bond, one for the bottom bond. It never grows with \(L\).

To compute energies from the tensors, the Hamiltonian must also be a chain that can sit between the MPS and its conjugate. An MPS writes a state as a chain of tensors; a matrix product operator (MPO) does the same for an operator. Each site gets a 4-index tensor:

W[w_left, w_right, out, in]

It has two bond legs that link it to its neighbours, like an MPS tensor, and two spin legs: one joins the ket \(|\psi\rangle\) below (in), the other the bra \(\langle\psi|\) above (out). A useful way to read it: a small matrix in the bond indices whose entries are \(2\times2\) operators on that site. Multiplying these matrices along the chain gives \(H\); the next lesson shows how.

Why bother? DMRG never builds \(H\) as a \(2^L\times2^L\) matrix. It only ever contracts the MPO with the MPS, which is cheap.

An MPO with bond dimension 3 on 20 spins. How many numbers does it hold, compared with \(H\) as a matrix?

Each \(W\) is \(3\times3\times2\times2 = 36\) numbers, so at most \(20\times36 = 720\) (the end tensors are smaller). The matrix has \(4^{20}\approx 10^{12}\) entries.

A product operator such as \(Z_1Z_2\cdots Z_L\) is easy to write as an MPO: put one \(2\times2\) operator on each site. Nothing needs to pass between sites, so the bond dimension is 1.

The Hamiltonian is harder because it is a sum of about \(2L\) such products: one for each field term \(Z_i\) and one for each bond \(X_iX_{i+1}\). The obvious way to build a sum is to write each product as its own MPO and add them. But adding two MPOs puts their bonds side by side, so the bond dimensions add up. \(H\) would need bond dimension about \(2L\), growing with the chain.

In fact a single \(3\times3\) \(W\), the same on every site, gives \(H\) for any \(L\).

First, what multiplying \(W\)s means. \(W\) has four indices, W[a, b, out, in]. Fix the two bond indices \(a, b\) and what is left is a \(2\times2\) operator on that site's spin, \(W_{ab}\). So we can write \(W\) as a small matrix whose entries are operators, a matrix of matrices. Multiplying the \(W\)s of neighbouring sites then does two different things at once:

\((W_1W_2)_{ac} = \sum_b\, (W_1)_{ab} \otimes (W_2)_{bc}\)
  • The bond index \(b\) is summed, exactly as in ordinary matrix multiplication.
  • The operator entries are not multiplied with each other. They act on different spins, so they sit side by side as a tensor product: "this operator on site 1, that one on site 2".

The field term. Start with \(H = -h\sum_i Z_i\) alone, and take

\(W = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & -hZ \\ 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix}\)

The first site keeps only the top row and the last site only the right column. For three sites:

\(\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & -hZ_1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & -hZ_2 \\ 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & -hZ_1 - hZ_2 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & -hZ_1 - hZ_2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -hZ_3 \\ \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix} = -hZ_1 - hZ_2 - hZ_3\)

The full TFIM. Add a middle row and column for the bond term:

\(W = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & X & -hZ \\ 0 & 0 & -X \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix}\)

Again three sites, first site the top row, last site the right column:

\(\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & X_1 & -hZ_1 \end{pmatrix} W_2 = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & X_2 & -hZ_1 - hZ_2 - X_1X_2 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(\begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & X_2 & \cdots \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -hZ_3 \\ -X_3 \\ \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix} = -X_1X_2 - X_2X_3 - hZ_1 - hZ_2 - hZ_3\)

That is \(H\) for three sites. More sites work the same way.

In the code: dmrg.tfim_bulk_tensor(h) and dmrg.tfim_mpo(L, h).

Crosswhite and Bacon, arXiv:0708.1221; PRA 78, 012356 (2008).

On 4 sites, the term \(-X_2X_3\) comes from one entry of each \(W\). Which entries? Number rows and columns 0, 1, 2.

Site 1: \(W_{00} = \mathbb{1}\). Site 2: \(W_{01} = X\). Site 3: \(W_{12} = -X\). Site 4: \(W_{22} = \mathbb{1}\). Each term of \(H\) is one such chain of entries, with neighbouring column and row indices matching.

The size of the matrix \(W\) is the MPO bond dimension \(D\). DMRG's cost grows in proportion to \(D\) (see "Lanczos: matvecs only"), so it pays to know how big it is for other models:

  • TFIM: \(D = 3\).
  • Heisenberg \(\vec S_i\cdot\vec S_{i+1}\): \(D = 5\). Like the \(X\) row and column in the TFIM \(W\), each of \(S^x, S^y, S^z\) needs its own middle row and column.
  • Exponentially decaying \(\sum_{i<j} e^{-(j-i)/\xi} X_iX_j\): still \(D = 3\). Set \(W_{11} = e^{-1/\xi}\,\mathbb{1}\) and \(W_{12} = e^{-1/\xi}X\), with \(W_{01} = X\) as before. Now an \(X\) can travel along the middle entry for any number of sites before it is completed, picking up one factor \(e^{-1/\xi}\) per site.

Power-law interactions, such as \(1/r^3\) dipolar couplings, have no exact finite MPO. People fit them by a sum of a few exponentials, each costing one extra middle row and column.

What changes in the TFIM MPO if you add a longitudinal field \(-g\sum_i X_i\)?

Only the corner entry: \(W_{0,2} = -hZ - gX\). Every single-site term goes in that top-right entry.

DMRG is an approximation, so we want exact answers to measure its error against. The TFIM is one of the few models that has them. The Jordan–Wigner transformation rewrites the spins as fermions that do not interact with each other (Lieb, Schultz and Mattis 1961; Pfeuty 1970), and non-interacting fermions are solved by diagonalizing an \(L\times L\) matrix instead of a \(2^L\times2^L\) one. That gives exact energies at any size.

Open chain of \(L\) sites: build an \(L\times L\) matrix \(M\) with \(h\) on the diagonal and 1 just above it. For \(L = 4\):

\(M = \begin{pmatrix} h & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & h & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & h & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & h \end{pmatrix}\)

With \(s_n\) its singular values,

\(E_0 = -\sum_n s_n\)

Infinite chain, energy per site:

\(e_0 = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1 + h^2 - 2h\cos k}\;dk, \qquad e_0(h{=}1) = -\tfrac{4}{\pi}\)

In the code: dmrg.tfim_open_energy and dmrg.tfim_infinite_energy_density.

Check the open-chain formula for \(L = 2\) (take \(h > 0\)): find \(E_0\) from the singular values of \(M\), then by diagonalizing \(H = -X_1X_2 - h(Z_1 + Z_2)\) directly. Hint: \((s_1 + s_2)^2 = s_1^2 + s_2^2 + 2s_1s_2 = \operatorname{tr}(M^{\mathsf T}M) + 2|\det M|\).

\(M = \begin{pmatrix} h & 1 \\ 0 & h \end{pmatrix}\), so \(\operatorname{tr}(M^{\mathsf T}M) = 1 + 2h^2\) and \(\det M = h^2\). Then \((s_1+s_2)^2 = 1 + 4h^2\) and \(E_0 = -\sqrt{1+4h^2}\). Directly: \(X_1X_2\) only mixes \(|00\rangle \leftrightarrow |11\rangle\) and \(|01\rangle \leftrightarrow |10\rangle\). On \(\{|00\rangle, |11\rangle\}\), \(H = \begin{pmatrix} -2h & -1 \\ -1 & 2h \end{pmatrix}\), with eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt{1+4h^2}\). On \(\{|01\rangle, |10\rangle\}\) the eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). The lowest is \(-\sqrt{1+4h^2}\), as the formula says.

We now have every ingredient: the state as an MPS, \(H\) as an MPO, and exact energies to check against. DMRG is a variational method: among all MPS with bond dimension \(\chi\), find the one with the lowest energy

\(E = \frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\)

Note the difference from the hand example in unit 2. There we cut up a state we already had. DMRG cannot do that, because the full ground state is the \(2^L\) table we can never store. It has to find the MPS directly.

Optimizing all tensors at once is hard: the state is a product of the tensors, so the energy is a high-degree polynomial in them and can have local minima. DMRG does the next best thing: optimize one or two neighbouring tensors with all the others fixed, then move on, and sweep back and forth until nothing changes.

Each local problem is solved exactly (an eigenproblem, see "The effective Hamiltonian"), and the current tensor is one of the candidates, so the solve never raises the energy. Two-site DMRG then truncates the bond back to \(\chi\), which can raise it slightly, by about the discarded weight. So the energy goes down sweep after sweep and settles.

It settles where no one- or two-site change helps, which need not be the global minimum: DMRG can get stuck, for example from a poor starting state. No proof says it finds the ground state. In the worst case no fast method can (unless P = NP), since finding the best MPS is NP-hard even for some 1D Hamiltonians whose ground states are exact MPS (Eisert, arXiv:quant-ph/0609051; Schuch, Cirac and Verstraete, arXiv:0802.3351). On physical models it almost always works, and for the TFIM we can check every answer exactly.

White, PRL 69, 2863 (1992); the MPS view: Schollwöck, arXiv:1008.3477, Sec. 6.

Is the DMRG energy an upper bound or a lower bound on the true ground energy?

An upper bound, as for any variational state. That is why the errors \(E_\text{DMRG} - E_\text{exact}\) in this course are always positive.

The energy \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\) is a three-row ladder: the MPS on top, the MPO in the middle, the conjugate MPS below, zipped up just like the overlap. Fix the two sites \(i, i{+}1\) that we want to optimize. Everything else does not change during that step, so it can be contracted ahead of time into two blocks:

  • the left environment L[ket, mpo, bra]: sites \(1\ldots i{-}1\), whose MPS tensors are left-canonical \(A\)s,
  • the right environment R[ket, mpo, bra]: sites \(i{+}2\ldots L\), whose MPS tensors are right-canonical \(B\)s.

In the picture the two highlighted columns are the sites being optimized. Everything inside the dashed boxes is contracted once and stored.

When the optimization moves one site to the right, the new left environment is the old one plus one site, a single small contraction. So DMRG caches all environments and updates them as it sweeps.

In the code: grow_left(L, A, W) and grow_right(R, B, W) in dmrg/finite.py.

What shape is a left environment at a bond of dimension \(\chi\), for the TFIM?

\(\chi \times 3 \times \chi\): ket bond, MPO bond (\(D = 3\)), bra bond.

Merge the two site tensors into one \(\theta[a, s_1, s_2, b]\). The MPS is now \(A_1\cdots A_{i-1}\,\theta\,B_{i+2}\cdots B_L\), in mixed canonical form. The energy is a ratio of two ladders, each with \(\theta\) in the middle:

  • The norm \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\) has no MPO. The \(A\)s collapse from the left and the \(B\)s from the right, as in "Why cancelling matters", leaving \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum |\theta|^2 = \theta^\dagger\theta\).
  • The energy \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\) has the MPO in the middle row, so nothing cancels. What is left and right of \(\theta\) is exactly \(L\) and \(R\): \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle = \theta^\dagger H_\text{eff}\,\theta\).

Minimizing \(\theta^\dagger H_\text{eff}\theta / \theta^\dagger\theta\) is an ordinary eigenvalue problem:

\(H_\text{eff}\,\theta = E\,\theta\)

\(H_\text{eff}\) is \(L\), \(W_i\), \(W_{i+1}\), \(R\) contracted together. In words: the left block offers \(\chi\) states for the left part of the chain, the right block \(\chi\) for the right part, and \(H_\text{eff}\) is the full Hamiltonian restricted to those states times the two free spins.

The canonical form is a property of the MPS tensors, not of \(L\) and \(R\), which contain the Hamiltonian. The sweep keeps the tensors canonical as it goes: each split keeps \(U\), a left-canonical \(A\), before that site is absorbed into \(L\). Without canonical form, the norm ladder would not collapse: it would be \(\theta^\dagger N\theta\) for some matrix \(N\), and you would have to solve the harder problem \(H\theta = E N\theta\), which becomes unstable when \(N\) is nearly singular.

How long is the vector \(\theta\) for \(\chi = 64\), \(d = 2\)?

\(\chi \cdot d \cdot d \cdot \chi = 64\cdot2\cdot2\cdot64 = 16\,384\).

The previous check gave a \(\theta\) with 16 384 entries, so a dense \(H_\text{eff}\) would have \(2.7\times10^8\). We never build it. The Lanczos method finds the lowest eigenvector using only matrix–vector products, or matvecs, \(\theta \mapsto H_\text{eff}\theta\). It applies \(H\) repeatedly to build the small space spanned by \(\theta, H\theta, H^2\theta, \ldots\) (the Krylov space) and diagonalizes \(H\) inside it. The lowest eigenvalue converges in a few dozen matvecs.

To apply \(H_\text{eff}\), follow the picture in "The effective Hamiltonian" one tensor at a time: join \(L\) to \(\theta\), then \(W_i\), then \(W_{i+1}\), then \(R\). Each step is one small contraction, so the cost is about \(D d^2 \chi^3\), far less than the \(d^4\chi^4\) of multiplying by a dense matrix.

Your datascipy's eigsh needed about 38 matvecs per local solve on the \(L = 40\) chain.

Why start Lanczos from the current \(\theta\) rather than a random vector?

After the first sweep the current \(\theta\) is already close to the answer, so Lanczos converges in far fewer steps.

Lanczos returns the best \(\theta\), but it is one tensor covering two sites, and the MPS needs one tensor per site. So split it back into two sites with an SVD. This is the same move you did by hand in "Step 2: split off the first spin": reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), pass \(SV^\dagger\) on. The difference is that DMRG never has the whole state to cut up. It only ever splits this one small two-site tensor, which it just found by minimizing the energy.

\(\theta_{(a s_1),(s_2 b)} = \sum_{k=1}^{\chi} U_{(a s_1),k}\, S_k\, V^\dagger_{k,(s_2 b)}\)

In the picture, the row index \((a s_1)\) of \(U\) is split back into its two legs \(a\) and \(s_1\), so \(U\) becomes a three-leg tensor \(U[a, s_1, k]\). Its columns are orthonormal, so it is left-canonical: a triangle pointing right, the same as an \(A\) (see "Tensors that cancel"). In the same way, \(V^\dagger[k, s_2, b]\) has orthonormal rows, so it is right-canonical, a \(B\).

Keep at most \(\chi\) singular values. This is where the approximation happens, and the discarded weight tells you how big it is. Sweeping right, \(U\) becomes the new \(A_i\) and \(SV^\dagger\) moves into site \(i{+}1\). Sweeping left, \(V^\dagger\) becomes the new \(B_{i+1}\) and \(US\) moves into site \(i\).

Optimizing two sites lets the bond between them grow, up to \(\chi\). One-site DMRG cannot grow bonds on its own and needs a "mixer" (White, arXiv:cond-mat/0508709).

Right after the split, where is the orthogonality centre?

On site \(i{+}1\), which holds \(SV^\dagger\). That is exactly where the next two-site step (sites \(i{+}1, i{+}2\)) needs it.

Now put the steps together. One sweep: optimize bonds \((1,2), (2,3), \ldots, (L{-}1, L)\), then come back. Each step updates one environment. After a few sweeps the energy stops changing.

The animation below runs one full sweep on 6 sites. It shows the energy ladder \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\) with the stored environments boxed, and under each site the blocks DMRG has stored so far.

for i in range(L - 1):        # left to right
    theta = A[i] @ A[i+1]
    E, theta = solve(Lenv[i], W[i], W[i+1], Renv[i+2], theta)
    A[i], A[i+1] = split(theta)      # A[i] left-canonical
    Lenv[i+1] = grow_left(Lenv[i], A[i], W[i])
# then the same from right to left
Your data\(L = 40\), \(h = 1\), \(\chi = 32\): the energy is \(-50.56943379\ldots\) after sweep 2 and agrees with the exact result to \(10^{-13}\). About 10 seconds.

Why start from a right-canonical MPS?

So that all right environments can be built at once before the first left-to-right half sweep, and every block to the right of the centre is an orthonormal basis.

When no exact answer exists, use the discarded weight. The energy error is roughly proportional to it, so you can run several \(\chi\) values and extrapolate the energy to zero discarded weight.

Your data\(L = 40\), \(h = 1\). Energy error from \(\chi = 2\) to 16: \(7\times10^{-2}\), \(2\times10^{-3}\), \(5\times10^{-5}\), \(1\times10^{-5}\), \(7\times10^{-8}\), \(1\times10^{-9}\). On a log–log plot against the discarded weight, it is a straight line. Notebook 3.

Also check that the energy has stopped changing between sweeps, and that observables such as \(S(l)\) are stable as \(\chi\) grows.

Each local solve takes Lanczos about 38 matvecs \(\theta \mapsto H_\text{eff}\theta\). One matvec contracts \(\theta\) (legs \(\chi, d, d, \chi\)) with \(L\) (legs \(\chi, D, \chi\)), the two \(W\)s and \(R\), one tensor at a time, and each contraction costs the product of the sizes of all the indices it touches. If you double \(\chi\), about how many times longer does one matvec take?

About 8. Attaching \(L\) touches three different \(\chi\) indices (two on \(L\), two on \(\theta\), one pair summed), so it costs \(\chi^3 D d^2\); attaching \(R\) is the same. The \(W\) steps touch only two, \(\chi^2 D^2 d^3\). Doubling \(\chi\) multiplies the \(\chi^3\) steps by \(2^3 = 8\). Lanczos needs about as many matvecs as before, so a whole sweep is also about 8 times slower.

DMRG is not only for energies. Here it measures the central charge \(c\) from "The area law". For an open critical chain, conformal field theory predicts how the entropy depends on the cut position \(l\) (Calabrese and Cardy, arXiv:hep-th/0405152):

\(S(l) = \frac{c}{6}\log_2\!\Big[\frac{2L}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi l}{L}\Big] + \text{const}\)

DMRG gives \(S(l)\) for every cut for free, from the Schmidt values of the last sweep. A straight-line fit against the bracket gives \(c\). The Ising transition has \(c = 1/2\).

Your data\(L = 100\), \(h = 1\), \(\chi = 32\): the fit over all cuts gives \(c \approx 0.53\). The excess comes from the few cuts nearest the open ends, which sit below the line: the formula holds only for blocks much longer than one site. Leaving out 10 cuts at each end gives \(c \approx 0.51\).

One dot per cut (cuts \(l\) and \(L - l\) coincide). From results/critical_entropy.json.

For a periodic chain the prefactor is \(c/3\), not \(c/6\). Why?

A block in a ring has two boundaries with the rest of the system; a block at the end of an open chain has one. Each boundary contributes \(c/6\).

At \(h = 0.5\) exact diagonalization of 12 spins gives a cat state, an equal superposition of "mostly along \(+x\)" and "mostly along \(-x\)", with \(S \approx 1\) bit, where \(S\) is the entanglement entropy of the middle cut (see "Entanglement entropy"; not the Schmidt values). DMRG at \(L = 100\) gives \(S \approx 0\). Which is right?

Both are ground states, almost. In the ordered phase the symmetric cat and the two symmetry-broken states (all spins along \(+x\) or \(-x\), dressed by fluctuations) are degenerate up to a splitting of order \(h^L\). Exact diagonalization picks the true, symmetric one. DMRG picks a symmetry-broken one, because truncation favours low entanglement and the energy difference is far below its precision.

This is spontaneous symmetry breaking showing up in a numerical method.

How could you make DMRG return the cat state?

Enforce the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) parity \(\prod_i Z_i\) in the tensors (symmetric DMRG), so only parity-even states are allowed.

Infinite DMRG (iDMRG) finds the ground state of an infinitely long chain by growing a finite chain from the middle, two sites per step, until the middle no longer feels the edges. It starts from a chain you can solve by hand: two spins.

Step 1: two spins. \(H = -X_1X_2 - h(Z_1 + Z_2)\). As an MPO ("Building the TFIM MPO") site 1 keeps only the top row of \(W\), so it has no left leg, and site 2 keeps only the right column, so it has no right leg. The ground state \(\theta[s_1, s_2]\) has just the two spin legs, 4 entries, and is found exactly:

Adding \(L\) and \(R\). To grow the chain, every site should carry the same full \(W\), with both bond legs. So give both sites the full \(W\) and close the extra legs at the ends. On the left, \(L = (1, 0, 0)\) closes the MPO leg and keeps the top row; on the right, \(R = (0, 0, 1)^T\) keeps the right column. \(\theta\) gets bond legs of size 1 at both ends, \(\theta[a, s_1, s_2, b]\) with \(a = b = 0\), and \(L\), \(R\) close those with the number 1. Nothing changes: it is the same \(H\) and the same \(\theta\).

So \(L\) and \(R\) start as \(1 \times 3 \times 1\) arrays, a \(\delta\) that picks the first or the last MPO state:

L = np.zeros((1, 3, 1)); L[0, 0, 0] = 1
R = np.zeros((1, 3, 1)); R[0, 2, 0] = 1

Grow the blocks. Split \(\theta = A\,S\,B\) with the SVD and draw the same ladder with it, and its conjugate below. Then regroup: \(L\) with the column \(A\), \(W\), \(A^*\) is the new \(L\), and the column \(B\), \(W\), \(B^*\) with \(R\) is the new \(R\). Each is a \(2 \times 3 \times 2\) block, standing for one site. \(S\) is left in the middle; step 2 replaces it with a new \(\theta\).

In the code: the start of dmrg.idmrg, then grow_left(L, A, W) and grow_right(R, B, W).

White, PRL 69, 2863 (1992); McCulloch, arXiv:0804.2509.

Why is the step-1 energy at \(h = 1\) equal to \(-\sqrt5\)?

\(X_1X_2\) flips both spins, so the ground state lives on \(|00\rangle, |11\rangle\), where \(Z_1 + Z_2 = +2, -2\). There \(H = \begin{pmatrix} -2h & -1 \\ -1 & 2h \end{pmatrix}\), with lowest eigenvalue \(-\sqrt{1 + 4h^2} = -\sqrt5\).

Step 2 is step 1 with bigger blocks. Between \(L\) and \(R\), in place of \(S\), put two new columns: a new two-site \(\theta[a, s_1, s_2, b]\), whose bond legs \(a, b\) match \(L\) and \(R\) (size 2 here), two \(W\)s below it and \(\theta^*\) at the bottom. Then solve for \(\theta\), split and absorb, as in step 1.

Every later step is the same loop:

for step in range(n_steps):
    E, theta = solve(L, W, W, R, theta0)
    A, S, B = split(theta, chi_max)
    L = grow_left(L, A, W)    # absorb A
    R = grow_right(R, B, W)   # absorb B

The solve is the finite-DMRG one ("The effective Hamiltonian"), and \(E\) is the total energy of all \(N = 2n\) sites after \(n\) steps. The difference is that there are no sweeps: once a site is absorbed its tensor never changes again, so the blocks are the memory.

What grows is the size. \(\theta\) reshapes to a \(2\chi \times 2\chi\) matrix, so the bond dimension can at most double per step. With \(\chi = 16\):

Up to step 5 the split drops nothing, and \(\theta\) holds all \(2^N\) amplitudes. At step 2, for example, \(L\)'s 2 states span site 1 completely, so \(\theta\)'s 16 entries are the whole 4-spin space. These steps are exact diagonalization, in a basis iDMRG builds as it goes. From step 5's split on, the SVD keeps 16 of 32 Schmidt values and the answer is approximate. It is the same loop throughout: reaching \(\chi\) only means the split starts truncating.

With \(\chi = 32\) instead, what is the last step that is still exact?

Step 6. The bond dimension is 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 after steps 1–5, so step 6's \(\theta\) has \(32 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 32 = 4096 = 2^{12}\) entries, all of the 12-spin space. Its split is the first truncation, 64 down to 32.

Watch two numbers from step to step: the energy per site and the entanglement entropy of the centre bond. While the chain is short both drift, because the centre still feels the edges. When they stop changing, the centre describes the infinite chain. The tensors absorbed early were found in a short chain, but by then they are far from the centre and no longer matter.

At that point the \(A\) just absorbed equals the one absorbed before it (up to gauge), and the same holds for \(B\). The infinite chain reads \(\cdots A\,A\,A\,S\,B\,B\,B \cdots\), so a few tensors describe infinitely many sites.

Your data\(h = 1.5\), \(\chi = 16\), 40 steps (80 sites): \(e = -1.67192622153622\), against the exact \(-1.67192622153619\). Notebook 4.

The chain has to grow well beyond the correlation length before the centre settles. At \(h = 1.5\) the correlation length is short, so 40 steps are enough. At \(h = 1\) the notebook needs 1000 steps (2000 sites).

Each step gives the total energy \(E_N\), which includes edge effects. How do you get the energy per site?

From the growth: \(e = (E_{N+2} - E_N)/2\). The edges contribute the same at every step once converged, so they cancel.

How far apart can two spins be and still be correlated? That distance is the correlation length \(\xi\), and an infinite MPS gives it directly. An MPS that repeats the same tensors along the chain has a transfer matrix: one unit cell of ket and bra contracted over the physical legs, \(T = \sum_s B^s \otimes \overline{B^s}\). Correlations at distance \(r\) behave like \(T^r\). For a normalized state the largest eigenvalue is 1, and it gives the uncorrelated part. What is left, the connected correlation \(\langle O_0 O_r\rangle_c = \langle O_0 O_r\rangle - \langle O_0\rangle\langle O_r\rangle\), decays with the second-largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\):

\(\langle O_0 O_r\rangle_c \sim |\lambda_2|^{r}, \qquad \xi = -\frac{1}{\ln|\lambda_2|}\ \text{per unit cell}\)

In iDMRG \(B\) is the right tensor of the last split. The cell has two sites, so \(T\) is the product of two such matrices: one for \(B\) and one for the other site, written in the same right-canonical form (B1 = S_prev⁻¹ A S in the code). One \(T\) moves two sites, so \(\xi = -2/\ln|\lambda_2|\) in sites. (A length, so the natural log here.)

In the code: dmrg.correlation_length(result).

Your data\(h = 1.5\), \(\chi = 16\): \(\xi = 2.39\). Exact: \(1/\ln 1.5 = 2.47\). At \(h = 0.5\) you get \(\xi \approx 5\times10^9\): the cat state has a second eigenvalue equal to 1.

Why must \(\xi\) be finite for any MPS with finite \(\chi\)?

\(T\) is a finite \(\chi^2\times\chi^2\) matrix, so unless \(|\lambda_2| = 1\) exactly, correlations decay exponentially.

At a critical point the true \(\xi\) is infinite, but any MPS with finite \(\chi\) has a finite one (previous check). So a finite \(\chi\) cuts off correlations much as a finite chain length would, and \(\chi\) plays the role of a system size. Two scaling laws follow (Tagliacozzo et al. arXiv:0801.2970; Pollmann et al. arXiv:0812.2903):

\(S = \frac{c}{6}\log_2\xi + \text{const}, \qquad \xi \propto \chi^{\kappa},\;\; \kappa \approx 2.03 \text{ for } c = \tfrac12\)
Your data\(h = 1\), \(\chi = 6\) to 16: \(\xi\) grows from 55 to 434 sites, and the fit of \(S\) against \(\log_2\xi\) gives \(c \approx 0.51\). Each run needed 1000 steps (2000 sites) to converge.

Why does iDMRG at criticality need so many steps?

The chain has to grow well beyond \(\xi(\chi)\), which is hundreds of sites here, before the centre forgets the edges.

  • Symmetries. Block-sparse tensors labelled by conserved charges. Faster, and they fix puzzles like the cat state.
  • Single-site DMRG with a mixer or subspace expansion (Hubig et al. arXiv:1501.05504).
  • 2D systems on cylinders, snaking the MPS through the lattice (Stoudenmire and White arXiv:1105.1374).
  • A better start for iDMRG. McCulloch's wavefunction prediction gives Lanczos a starting vector built from the last step, about three times faster in notebook 4 (arXiv:0804.2509).
  • Time evolution with TEBD or TDVP (Haegeman et al. arXiv:1408.5056).
  • Excited states by orthogonality penalties, and the tangent-space excitation ansatz.
