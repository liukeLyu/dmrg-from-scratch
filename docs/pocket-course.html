A quantum state of \(L\) spins-1/2 is a list of \(2^L\) complex amplitudes \(\psi_{s_1 s_2\cdots s_L}\). Each new spin doubles the list.

\(L = 50 \;\Rightarrow\; 2^{50} \approx 10^{15}\) amplitudes \(\approx 18\) petabytes

Exact diagonalization stops around \(L \approx 40\) no matter how big the computer. To go further we need a way to store only the part of \(\psi\) that matters.

DMRG's bet: ground states of local Hamiltonians are special. Almost all of those \(2^L\) numbers are redundant, and the redundancy is measured by entanglement.

How much memory does a complex state vector of 30 spins need, in double precision?

\(2^{30} \approx 1.07\times10^9\) amplitudes × 16 bytes ≈ 17 GB. Doable on a big node, but 40 spins would need 17 TB.

Cut the chain into a left part and a right part. Reshape \(\psi\) into a matrix: rows label the left spins, columns the right spins. Its singular value decomposition is

\(|\psi\rangle = \sum_a S_a\, |a\rangle_L\, |a\rangle_R, \qquad S_1 \ge S_2 \ge \dots \ge 0,\;\; \sum_a S_a^2 = 1\)

The \(|a\rangle_L\) and \(|a\rangle_R\) are orthonormal states of each half. The singular values \(S_a\) are the Schmidt values. They tell you everything about the entanglement across that cut.

One nonzero \(S_a\) means a product state: no entanglement. Many comparable \(S_a\) means strong entanglement.

In the code: np.linalg.svd(psi.reshape(2**l, -1)), used inside dmrg.mps.from_dense.

What are the Schmidt values of the Bell pair \((|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt2\)?

Two equal values, \(S_1 = S_2 = 1/\sqrt2\). The matrix is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\mathbb{1}\).

Square the Schmidt values to get probabilities \(p_a = S_a^2\). The entanglement entropy of the cut is their Shannon entropy:

\(S = -\sum_a p_a \log_2 p_a\)

We use base 2, so entropy is measured in bits: a Bell pair carries exactly 1 bit of entanglement.

It is the von Neumann entropy of either half's reduced density matrix, whose eigenvalues are exactly the \(p_a\). If only \(\chi\) Schmidt values are nonzero, \(S \le \log_2 \chi\). Turn that around: a state with entropy \(S\) needs at least about \(2^{S}\) Schmidt values to be described well.

In the code: dmrg.mps.entropy(S).

The GHZ state \((|00\cdots0\rangle + |11\cdots1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) of 10 spins. What is the entropy of any cut?

1 bit for every cut, because there are always exactly two Schmidt values \(1/\sqrt2\). Lots of spins, very little entanglement per cut.

A random state has entropy close to the maximum, \(L/2\) bits, at the middle cut. That is the volume law, and it is hopeless to compress.

Ground states of gapped, local 1D Hamiltonians are different. Their entropy stays bounded as \(L\) grows: only spins near the cut are entangled across it. This is the 1D area law (Hastings, arXiv:0705.2024).

At a critical point the entropy grows, but only logarithmically. For a block of \(l\) sites in an infinite chain, \(S = \tfrac{c}{3}\log_2 l\), with \(c\) the central charge of the CFT.

Your dataOpen TFIM chain, \(L = 100\): the middle-cut entropy is about 0.84 bits at \(h = 1\), 0.22 bits at \(h = 1.5\), and almost 0 at \(h = 0.5\). Notebook 3.

Why does the area law make ground states compressible?

Bounded entropy means the Schmidt values decay fast, so a fixed number \(\chi\) of them captures the state at every cut, independent of \(L\).

If the Schmidt values decay fast, throw away the small ones. Keep the largest \(\chi\) and renormalize. The squared error of the state is the weight you discarded:

\(\varepsilon = \sum_{a > \chi} S_a^2\)

This is the best possible rank-\(\chi\) approximation of that cut (the Eckart–Young theorem). \(\chi\) is called the bond dimension, and it is the single knob that controls DMRG's accuracy and cost.

Your dataGround state of 12 spins at \(h = 1\), full \(\chi = 64\). Keeping \(\chi = 4\) gives infidelity \(6\times10^{-6}\). Keeping \(\chi = 8\) gives \(9\times10^{-10}\). Notebook 1.

The Schmidt values squared are 0.9, 0.09, 0.009, 0.001. What is the error if you keep \(\chi = 2\)?

\(\varepsilon = 0.009 + 0.001 = 0.01\), so fidelity about 0.99.

From here on we draw tensors as pictures. There are only two rules. A shape is a tensor, a block of numbers. Each line sticking out of it is one index: a vector has one leg, a matrix two, a 3-index tensor three.

Joining two legs means summing over that index. Two matrices joined by a leg are their matrix product, \((AB)_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}\):

The open legs that remain tell you what the result is. A state of three spins is one tensor with three open legs, one per spin:

Later lessons give shapes extra meaning: diamonds hold Schmidt values, triangles are tensors in canonical form, squares are pieces of the Hamiltonian, and a thick line is several indices grouped into one.

What do you get if you join the two legs of a matrix to each other?

Its trace, \(\sum_i M_{ii}\). No open legs are left, so the result is a single number.

We will build a matrix product state by hand for a real ground state: three spins of the transverse-field Ising chain \(H = -\sum_i Z_iZ_{i+1} - h\sum_i X_i\) at the critical point \(h = 1\).

A state of three spins is 8 numbers \(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3}\), one for each pattern. Rounded to three digits, the ground state is:

\(\begin{array}{cccccccc} 000 & 001 & 010 & 011 & 100 & 101 & 110 & 111 \\ 0.532 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.532 \end{array}\)

Our goal over the next few lessons: rewrite these 8 numbers as three small pieces, one per spin, that multiply together to give back any \(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3}\) we ask for. Those pieces are the MPS.

Why is \(\psi_{000} = \psi_{111}\), and \(\psi_{001} = \psi_{110}\)?

Flipping every spin, \(\prod_i X_i\), commutes with \(H\), and the ground state is symmetric under it. So each pattern and its flipped partner have the same amplitude.

Arrange the 8 numbers in a grid. Let the row be the first spin \(s_1\) (2 rows) and the column be the other two spins \(s_2 s_3\) (4 columns: 00, 01, 10, 11):

\(M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.532 & 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 \\ 0.296 & 0.204 & 0.296 & 0.532 \end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix} \leftarrow s_1 = 0 \\ \leftarrow s_1 = 1 \end{matrix}\)

Nothing was computed. These are the same 8 numbers, only laid out differently. In NumPy it is psi.reshape(2, 4).

Why bother? This matrix is exactly the cut between spin 1 and the rest. Its singular values are the Schmidt values of that cut (lesson 2), so the next step will tell us how strongly spin 1 is entangled with spins 2 and 3.

Which entry of \(M\) holds \(\psi_{110}\)?

Row \(s_1 = 1\), column \(s_2 s_3 = 10\): bottom row, third entry, 0.296.

Take the SVD, \(M = U\,S\,V^\dagger\). A computer gives

\(U = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0.967 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.254 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(V^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.605 & 0.366 & 0.366 & 0.605 \\ 0.660 & 0.254 & -0.254 & -0.660 \end{pmatrix}\)

Multiplying \(S\) into \(V^\dagger\) scales its rows by the Schmidt values:

\(SV^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.585 & 0.354 & 0.354 & 0.585 \\ 0.168 & 0.064 & -0.064 & -0.168 \end{pmatrix}\)

Look at what each factor depends on. \(U\) has rows labelled by \(s_1\) only. \(SV^\dagger\) has columns labelled by \(s_2 s_3\) only. They are glued by a new, summed index \(a\), the column of \(U\) and the row of \(SV^\dagger\):

\(\psi_{s_1, s_2 s_3} = \sum_{a=1}^{2} U_{s_1 a}\,\big(SV^\dagger\big)_{a,\, s_2 s_3}\)

Keep \(U\) as the tensor of spin 1. The index \(a\) is a bond: the only channel through which spin 1 talks to the rest. The Schmidt values 0.967 and 0.254 say spin 1 is entangled, but not much: 0.34 bits.

If we kept only the largest Schmidt value, what squared error would this cut make?

The discarded weight \(0.254^2 \approx 0.065\) (lesson 5). The bond would shrink from 2 to 1.

What is left is \(R = SV^\dagger\), with rows \(a\) and columns \(s_2 s_3\). Do the same trick again, but now let the row be the pair \((a, s_2)\) and the column be \(s_3\):

\(R' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.585 & 0.354 \\ 0.354 & 0.585 \\ 0.168 & 0.064 \\ -0.064 & -0.168 \end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix} \leftarrow a{=}1,\, s_2{=}0 \\ \leftarrow a{=}1,\, s_2{=}1 \\ \leftarrow a{=}2,\, s_2{=}0 \\ \leftarrow a{=}2,\, s_2{=}1 \end{matrix}\)

Again the same 8 numbers, rearranged. SVD again, \(R' = U' S' V'^\dagger\):

\(U' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & 0.646 \\ 0.686 & -0.646 \\ 0.170 & 0.288 \\ -0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S' = \begin{pmatrix} 0.967 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.254 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(V'^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S'V'^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 & 0.684 \\ 0.179 & -0.179 \end{pmatrix}\)

A new bond \(b\) of size 2 appears between spins 2 and 3. Keep \(U'\) as the tensor of spin 2. It has three indices: the old bond \(a\) on its left, its own spin \(s_2\), and the new bond \(b\) on its right. The leftover \(S'V'^\dagger\) is the tensor of spin 3, with indices \(b\) and \(s_3\).

In the code this loop is dmrg.from_dense: reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), carry \(SV^\dagger\) on.

The new Schmidt values \(S'\) are again 0.967 and 0.254. Why the same as at the first cut?

The cut between spins 2 and 3 is the mirror image of the cut between spins 1 and 2, and the open 3-site chain is symmetric under reflection.

We now have one piece per spin. Fixing the spin value turns each piece into a small matrix. Rows of \(U\) give spin 1; the rows of \(U'\) with \(s_2 = 0\) or \(1\) give spin 2; the columns of \(S'V'^\dagger\) give spin 3:

\(A_1^{0} = (0.707\;\;0.707),\qquad A_1^{1} = (0.707\;\;{-0.707})\)
\(A_2^{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & 0.646 \\ 0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix},\qquad A_2^{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.686 & -0.646 \\ -0.170 & 0.288 \end{pmatrix}\)
\(A_3^{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 \\ 0.179 \end{pmatrix},\qquad A_3^{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.684 \\ -0.179 \end{pmatrix}\)

To get any amplitude, pick the matrix matching each spin and multiply them in order:

\(\psi_{s_1 s_2 s_3} = A_1^{s_1}\,A_2^{s_2}\,A_3^{s_3}\)

For \(\psi_{000}\): \((0.707\;\;0.707)\,A_2^0 = (0.605\;\;0.660)\), then \(0.605\times0.684 + 0.660\times0.179 = 0.532\). It matches the table. A row vector, times a matrix, times a column vector gives a number. That is why this is called a matrix product state.

Compute \(\psi_{010}\) the same way.

\((0.707\;\;0.707)\,A_2^{1} = (0.365\;\;{-0.253})\), then \(0.365\times0.684 - 0.253\times0.179 \approx 0.204\). It matches.

For \(L\) spins, repeat step 3 until one spin is left: reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), carry \(SV^\dagger\) to the right. You always end with

\(\psi_{s_1 s_2 \cdots s_L} = A_1^{s_1} A_2^{s_2} \cdots A_L^{s_L}\)

In code each site is one 3-index array A[left, phys, right]: left bond, spin, right bond. The first site's left bond and the last site's right bond have size 1, which is why the product comes out as a number.

Notice that our 3-spin MPS holds \(4 + 8 + 4 = 16\) numbers (\(A_1\) is \(2\times2\), \(A_2\) is \(2\times2\times2\), \(A_3\) is \(2\times2\)), twice the 8 we started with. Compression only appears for long chains, where two facts take over:

  • Done exactly, the bonds grow up to \(2^{L/2}\) in the middle.
  • At each SVD we can keep only the largest \(\chi\) Schmidt values (lesson 5). For ground states the discarded weight is tiny even at modest \(\chi\), so every bond stays small while \(L\) grows.

In the code: dmrg.from_dense(psi, L), and back with dmrg.to_dense(mps).

What are the bond sizes of an exact MPS for a random 8-spin state?

1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Each bond is limited by the smaller side of its cut, \(\min(2^l, 2^{L-l})\). A random state is volume-law entangled, so truncating it would lose a lot.

An MPS has \(L\) tensors of size at most \(\chi \times d \times \chi\), with \(d = 2\) for spins. So the storage is about

\(L\, d\, \chi^2 \quad\text{instead of}\quad d^{L}\)

Linear in \(L\) instead of exponential. The price is that not every state fits: only states whose entanglement is at most about \(\log_2\chi\) bits at every cut. By the area law, ground states do.

How many numbers does an MPS with \(L = 100\), \(d = 2\), \(\chi = 64\) hold?

\(100 \times 2 \times 64^2 \approx 8\times10^5\). Under 7 MB. The full vector would have \(2^{100} \approx 10^{30}\) entries.

Take any invertible matrix \(X\) and insert \(X X^{-1} = \mathbb{1}\) on a bond:

\(A_i \to A_i X, \qquad A_{i+1} \to X^{-1} A_{i+1}\)

The state does not change, but the tensors do. So the MPS of a state is far from unique. This looks like a nuisance. It is actually a tool: we can choose \(X\) to make the tensors as convenient as possible. Those convenient choices are the canonical forms, next.

If you multiply \(A_3\) by 2 and \(A_7\) by 1/2, does the state change?

No. Scalars commute through the product, so the factors cancel. It is the simplest gauge transformation.

Go back to the \(U\) you kept in Step 2:

\(U = \begin{pmatrix} 0.707 & 0.707 \\ 0.707 & -0.707 \end{pmatrix}\)

Its two columns are orthonormal. Each has length 1, \(0.707^2 + 0.707^2 = 1\), and they are perpendicular, \(0.707\times0.707 + 0.707\times(-0.707) = 0\). In matrix language, \(U^\dagger U = \mathbb{1}\).

Now draw it. Put \(U\) on top of its complex conjugate \(U^*\) and join their spin legs. Joining \(s_1\) sums over it, which is exactly \(U^\dagger U\). So the pair is the identity, a plain line from \(a\) to \(a'\):

We draw such tensors as triangles pointing right. A triangle joined to its mirror image collapses into a line.

This is not luck. Every \(U\) from an SVD has orthonormal columns, so every tensor the recipe kept, \(U\) and \(U'\), is a triangle. They are called left-canonical.

Is \(U'\) from Step 3 a triangle too? Its first column is \((0.686,\ 0.686,\ 0.170,\ -0.170)\). Square these four entries and add them: do you get 1?

Yes: \(0.686^2 + 0.686^2 + 0.170^2 + 0.170^2 = 0.471 + 0.471 + 0.029 + 0.029 = 1.00\). The second column, \((0.646,\ -0.646,\ 0.288,\ 0.288)\), also gives 1, and multiplying the two columns entry by entry and adding gives 0. So \(U'^\dagger U' = \mathbb{1}\). For \(U'\) the joined legs are its left bond \(a\) and its spin \(s_2\); only \(b\) stays open.

Measure something. How much does spin 3 point along \(x\)? That is \(\langle\psi|X_3|\psi\rangle\). As a diagram: the MPS on top, its conjugate below, all spin legs joined, with the operator \(X\) inserted on spin 3:

Start from the left. \(A_1\) and \(A_1^*\) are a triangle pair joined on their spin leg, so they collapse into a line:

Now \(A_2\) and \(A_2^*\) are joined on their left (by that line) and on their spin leg. Same pattern, so they collapse too:

Only spin 3's own tensor is left. With \(A_3^{0} = (0.684,\ 0.179)\) and \(A_3^{1} = (0.684,\ -0.179)\), and \(X\) swapping \(0 \leftrightarrow 1\):

\(\langle X_3\rangle = 2\,A_3^{0}\cdot A_3^{1} = 2\,(0.684^2 - 0.179^2) = 0.872\)

The exact value is 0.871; the gap is rounding. The first two spins never entered the arithmetic. This is why DMRG keeps its tensors canonical: anything measured at the non-triangle site only needs that site, because the rest of the chain cancels.

What do you get if you leave out \(X\) in the last picture?

The norm \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\): the sum of the squared entries of \(A_3\), \(0.684^2 + 0.684^2 + 0.179^2 + 0.179^2 = 1.00\).

Run the recipe from the right end instead: split off spin 3 first, keep \(V^\dagger\) as its tensor, and carry \(US\) to the left. Each kept \(V^\dagger\) has orthonormal rows, \(V^\dagger V = \mathbb{1}\).

We draw it as a triangle pointing left and call it \(B\). It cancels with its conjugate when they are joined from the right:

So the rule for both kinds is the same: a triangle cancels when you join it to its mirror image on every leg except the one it points toward.

You never need the full state to get canonical tensors. Sweep along any MPS and re-split each tensor with an SVD (or a cheaper QR decomposition), pushing the leftover into the next site.

In the code: dmrg.left_canonicalize and dmrg.right_canonicalize.

A right-canonical \(B\) has legs \(a\) (left), \(s\) (spin), \(b\) (right). Which legs do you join with \(B^*\) to make it cancel?

\(s\) and \(b\). The left leg \(a\), the side the triangle points to, stays open, and the pair becomes a line from \(a\) to \(a'\).

Make everything left of a bond left-canonical and everything right of it right-canonical:

\(|\psi\rangle = A_1 \cdots A_i \; C \; B_{i+1} \cdots B_L\)

Our 3-spin example is already in this form: \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are triangles and \(A_3 = S'V'^\dagger\) is the centre \(C\).

All the information about the state's norm and entanglement now sits in the small matrix \(C\) at the centre. Because both blocks are orthonormal bases, \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum |C_{ab}|^2\), and the singular values of \(C\) are the Schmidt values of that cut.

This is the form DMRG works in. It moves the centre site by site and always optimizes at the centre.

Why is it cheap to read off entanglement in this form?

You only need the SVD of the \(\chi\times\chi\) matrix \(C\), not anything involving the rest of the chain.

The overlap \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) of two MPS is a ladder: the tensors \(A\) of \(\psi\) on top, the conjugated tensors \(B^*\) of \(\phi\) below, spin legs joined:

Contracting everything at once would rebuild the \(2^L\) vector. Instead, zip the ladder up from the left, one rung at a time. Keep a small matrix \(E\) that holds everything contracted so far (the dashed box). It has two open legs: the bond sticking out of the top row, and the one sticking out of the bottom row.

Where does \(E\) start? The first tensors \(A_1\) and \(B_1\) have a left bond of size 1 (a leg with only one value, drawn or not). Before anything is contracted, \(E\) is the \(1\times1\) matrix \((1)\): it multiplies by one and changes nothing. With that start, the first rung uses the same step as every other rung, so no special case is needed for the edge.

One step of the zipper joins \(E\) to the next rung and gives a new \(E\):

In formulas, summing over the three joined legs \(x\), \(y\) and \(s\):

\(E^\text{new}_{ab} = \sum_{x,\,y,\,s} E_{xy}\; A^{s}_{xa}\; \overline{B^{s}_{yb}}\)

Each step costs about \(d\chi^3\), so the whole overlap costs \(L d \chi^3\) instead of \(2^L\). At the right end both open bonds have size 1, and \(E\) is a single number: the overlap. Expectation values work the same way with an operator in the middle (lesson 20 onward).

In the code: dmrg.overlap.

What size is \(E\) in the middle of the chain, if every bond has dimension \(\chi\)?

\(\chi \times \chi\): one leg for the top bond, one for the bottom bond. It never grows with \(L\).

An MPS writes a state as a chain of tensors. A matrix product operator (MPO) does the same for an operator such as the Hamiltonian. Each site gets a 4-index tensor:

W[w_left, w_right, out, in]

Two physical legs (bra side and ket side) and two bond legs. Read it as a small matrix in the bond indices whose entries are \(2\times2\) operators. The product of these matrices along the chain gives \(H\).

Why bother? DMRG never builds \(H\) as a \(2^L\times2^L\) matrix. It only ever contracts the MPO with the MPS, which is cheap.

How many legs does a site tensor of \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\) have in total, counting MPS, MPO and conjugate MPS?

3 + 4 + 3 = 10 legs, but the physical ones are contracted in pairs, leaving three horizontal bonds (ket, MPO, bra) on each side.

For \(H = -\sum_i X_iX_{i+1} - h\sum_i Z_i\), read the chain left to right with a 3-state machine. State 0: nothing placed yet. State 1: an \(X\) was just placed, the next site must close it. State 2: a term is finished.

\(W = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb{1} & X & -hZ \\ 0 & 0 & -X \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbb{1} \end{pmatrix}\)

Row = state before the site, column = state after. Multiply \(W W \cdots W\) and keep the corner entry (0, 2). Every path from 0 to 2 is one term of \(H\). The first site keeps only row 0 and the last only column 2.

In the code: dmrg.tfim_bulk_tensor(h) and dmrg.tfim_mpo(L, h).

Which path through the machine produces the term \(-hZ_3\) on a 5-site chain?

Stay in 0 on sites 1 and 2 (identities), jump 0→2 on site 3 (picking \(-hZ\)), stay in 2 on sites 4 and 5.

The size of the machine is the MPO bond dimension \(D\). It sets part of DMRG's cost.

  • TFIM: \(D = 3\).
  • Heisenberg \(\vec S_i\cdot\vec S_{i+1}\): \(D = 5\) (one "open" state each for \(S^x, S^y, S^z\), or \(S^+, S^-, S^z\)).
  • Exponentially decaying \(\sum_{i<j} e^{-(j-i)/\xi} X_iX_j\): still \(D = 3\). The open state can loop on itself with weight \(e^{-1/\xi}\).

Power-law interactions have no exact finite MPO. People fit them by a sum of exponentials.

What changes in the TFIM machine if you add a longitudinal field \(-g\sum_i X_i\)?

Only the corner entry: \(W_{0,2} = -hZ - gX\). Single-site terms all live in the 0→2 jump.

The TFIM maps to free fermions by the Jordan–Wigner transformation (Lieb, Schultz and Mattis 1961; Pfeuty 1970). That gives exact energies to check DMRG at any size.

Open chain: let \(M\) be the \(L\times L\) matrix with \(h\) on the diagonal and 1 just above it. With \(s_n\) its singular values,

\(E_0 = -\sum_n s_n\)

Infinite chain, energy per site:

\(e_0 = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sqrt{1 + h^2 - 2h\cos k}\;dk, \qquad e_0(h{=}1) = -\tfrac{4}{\pi}\)

In the code: dmrg.tfim_open_energy and dmrg.tfim_infinite_energy_density.

Check the open-chain formula for \(L = 1\).

\(M = (h)\), so \(E_0 = -|h|\). That is right: \(H = -hZ\) has eigenvalues \(\pm h\).

DMRG is a variational method. Among all MPS with bond dimension \(\chi\), find the one with the lowest energy

\(E = \frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\)

Note the difference from lessons 7 to 12. There we cut up a state we already had. DMRG cannot do that, because the full ground state is the \(2^L\) table we can never store. It has to find the MPS directly.

Optimizing all tensors at once is a hard nonlinear problem. DMRG does the next best thing: optimize one or two neighbouring tensors with all the others fixed, then move on, and sweep back and forth until nothing changes. Each local step can only lower the energy.

White, PRL 69, 2863 (1992); the MPS view: Schollwöck, arXiv:1008.3477, Sec. 6.

Is the DMRG energy an upper bound or a lower bound on the true ground energy?

An upper bound, as for any variational state. That is why your errors \(E_\text{DMRG} - E_\text{exact}\) are always positive.

Fix the two sites \(i, i{+}1\) that we want to optimize. Everything else in \(\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\) can be contracted ahead of time into two blocks:

  • the left environment L[ket, mpo, bra]: sites \(1\ldots i{-}1\),
  • the right environment R[ket, mpo, bra]: sites \(i{+}2\ldots L\).

In the picture the two highlighted columns are the sites being optimized. Everything inside the dashed boxes is contracted once and stored.

When the optimization moves one site to the right, the new left environment is the old one plus one site, a single small contraction. So DMRG caches all environments and updates them as it sweeps.

In the code: grow_left(L, A, W) and grow_right(R, B, W) in dmrg/finite.py.

What shape is a left environment at a bond of dimension \(\chi\), for the TFIM?

\(\chi \times 3 \times \chi\): ket bond, MPO bond (\(D = 3\)), bra bond.

Merge the two site tensors into one \(\theta[a, s_1, s_2, b]\). The state is linear in \(\theta\). And because the left and right blocks are orthonormal bases (mixed canonical form), \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = |\theta|^2\). Minimizing the energy over \(\theta\) then becomes an ordinary eigenvalue problem:

\(H_\text{eff}\,\theta = E\,\theta\)

\(H_\text{eff}\) is \(L\), \(W_i\), \(W_{i+1}\), \(R\) contracted together: the full Hamiltonian, projected onto the basis the rest of the MPS defines.

Without canonical form you would get a generalized problem \(H\theta = E N\theta\) with a possibly ill-conditioned \(N\).

How long is the vector \(\theta\) for \(\chi = 64\), \(d = 2\)?

\(\chi \cdot d \cdot d \cdot \chi = 64\cdot2\cdot2\cdot64 = 16\,384\).

A dense \(H_\text{eff}\) of size \(16384^2\) would have \(2.7\times10^8\) entries. We never build it. The Lanczos method finds the lowest eigenvector using only the map \(\theta \mapsto H_\text{eff}\theta\). It builds the Krylov space \(\{\theta, H\theta, H^2\theta, \ldots\}\) and diagonalizes \(H\) inside it. Extreme eigenvalues converge in a few dozen steps.

To apply \(H_\text{eff}\), follow the picture in "The effective Hamiltonian" one tensor at a time: join \(L\) to \(\theta\), then \(W_i\), then \(W_{i+1}\), then \(R\). Each step is one small contraction, so the cost is about \(D d^2 \chi^3\), far less than the \(d^4\chi^4\) of multiplying by a dense matrix.

Your datascipy's eigsh needed about 38 matvecs per local solve on the \(L = 40\) chain.

Why start Lanczos from the current \(\theta\) rather than a random vector?

After the first sweep the current \(\theta\) is already close to the answer, so Lanczos converges in far fewer steps.

After solving for \(\theta\), split it back into two sites with an SVD. This is the same move you did by hand in "Step 2: split off the first spin": reshape, SVD, keep \(U\), pass \(SV^\dagger\) on. The difference is that DMRG never has the whole state to cut up. It only ever splits this one small two-site tensor, which it just found by minimizing the energy.

\(\theta_{(a s_1),(s_2 b)} = \sum_{k=1}^{\chi} U_{(a s_1),k}\, S_k\, V^\dagger_{k,(s_2 b)}\)

Keep at most \(\chi\) singular values. This is where the approximation happens, and the discarded weight tells you how big it is. Sweeping right, \(U\) becomes the new left-canonical \(A_i\) and \(SV^\dagger\) moves into site \(i{+}1\). Sweeping left, do the mirror image.

Optimizing two sites lets the bond between them grow, up to \(\chi\). One-site DMRG cannot grow bonds on its own and needs a "mixer" (White, arXiv:cond-mat/0508709).

Right after the split, where is the orthogonality centre?

On site \(i{+}1\), which holds \(SV^\dagger\). That is exactly where the next two-site step (sites \(i{+}1, i{+}2\)) needs it.

One sweep: optimize bonds \((1,2), (2,3), \ldots, (L{-}1, L)\), then come back. Each step updates one environment. After a few sweeps the energy stops changing.

for i in range(L - 1):        # left to right
    theta = A[i] @ A[i+1]
    E, theta = solve(Lenv[i], W[i], W[i+1], Renv[i+2], theta)
    A[i], A[i+1] = split(theta)      # A[i] left-canonical
    Lenv[i+1] = grow_left(Lenv[i], A[i], W[i])
# then the same from right to left
Your data\(L = 40\), \(h = 1\), \(\chi = 32\): the energy is \(-50.56943379\ldots\) after sweep 2 and agrees with the exact result to \(10^{-13}\). About 10 seconds.

Why start from a right-canonical MPS?

So that all right environments can be built at once before the first left-to-right half sweep, and every block to the right of the centre is an orthonormal basis.

When no exact answer exists, use the discarded weight. The energy error is roughly proportional to it, so you can run several \(\chi\) values and extrapolate the energy to zero discarded weight.

Your data\(L = 40\), \(h = 1\). Energy error from \(\chi = 2\) to 16: \(7\times10^{-2}\), \(2\times10^{-3}\), \(5\times10^{-5}\), \(1\times10^{-5}\), \(7\times10^{-8}\), \(1\times10^{-9}\). On a log–log plot against the discarded weight, it is a straight line. Notebook 3.

Also check that the energy has stopped changing between sweeps, and that observables such as \(S(l)\) are stable as \(\chi\) grows.

Doubling \(\chi\) multiplies the cost of each matvec by about how much?

About 8, since the cost scales as \(\chi^3\).

For an open critical chain, conformal field theory predicts how the entropy depends on the cut position \(l\) (Calabrese and Cardy, arXiv:hep-th/0405152):

\(S(l) = \frac{c}{6}\log_2\!\Big[\frac{2L}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi l}{L}\Big] + \text{const}\)

DMRG gives \(S(l)\) for every cut for free, from the Schmidt values of the last sweep. A straight-line fit against the bracket gives \(c\). The Ising transition has \(c = 1/2\).

Your data\(L = 100\), \(h = 1\), \(\chi = 32\): fit gives \(c \approx 0.53\). The small excess comes from even/odd oscillations near the open ends.

For a periodic chain the prefactor is \(c/3\), not \(c/6\). Why?

A block in a ring has two boundaries with the rest of the system; a block at the end of an open chain has one. Each boundary contributes \(c/6\).

At \(h = 0.5\) exact diagonalization of 12 spins gives a cat state, \(S \approx 1\) bit. DMRG at \(L = 100\) gives \(S \approx 0\). Which is right?

Both are ground states, almost. In the ordered phase the symmetric cat and the two symmetry-broken states (all spins along \(+x\) or \(-x\), dressed by fluctuations) are degenerate up to a splitting of order \(h^L\). Exact diagonalization picks the true, symmetric one. DMRG picks a symmetry-broken one, because truncation favours low entanglement and the energy difference is far below its precision.

This is spontaneous symmetry breaking showing up in a numerical method.

How could you make DMRG return the cat state?

Enforce the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) parity \(\prod_i Z_i\) in the tensors (symmetric DMRG), so only parity-even states are allowed.

Infinite DMRG (iDMRG) targets the infinite chain directly. Start with two sites. At every step insert two new sites in the middle, between the left and right blocks:

Solve the two-site problem exactly as in finite DMRG, split \(\theta = A\,S\,B\), absorb \(A\) into the left block and \(B\) into the right one. There are no sweeps: the environments are the memory. Once the centre stops changing, \(A\), \(S\), \(B\) describe the infinite chain.

McCulloch, arXiv:0804.2509.

The total energy \(E_N\) includes edge effects. How do you get the energy per site?

From the growth: \(e = (E_{N+2} - E_N)/2\). The edges contribute the same at every step once converged, so they cancel.

Each step needs a starting vector for Lanczos. The new centre looks like the old one shifted by one site. Written with the last step's tensors:

\(\theta_\text{guess} = S\,B\,S_\text{prev}^{-1}\,A\,S\)

where \(S_\text{prev}\) are the Schmidt values from the step before. It is one line of code and it saves most of the Lanczos iterations.

Your data300 steps at \(h = 1\), \(\chi = 16\): 12.4 s from random starts, 3.3 s with the guess, same energy. Notebook 4.

Why does the guess divide by \(S_\text{prev}\)?

To remove the old centre's weights before moving the centre over by one site, so the shifted state is correctly normalized in the new basis.

A translation-invariant MPS has a transfer matrix: one unit cell of ket and bra contracted over the physical legs, \(T = \sum_s B^s \otimes \overline{B^s}\). Correlations at distance \(r\) behave like \(T^r\). Its largest eigenvalue is 1, so

\(\langle O_0 O_r\rangle_c \sim |\lambda_2|^{r}, \qquad \xi = -\frac{1}{\ln|\lambda_2|}\ \text{per unit cell}\)

With two sites per cell, \(\xi = -2/\ln|\lambda_2|\) in sites. (A length, so the natural log here.)

Your data\(h = 1.5\), \(\chi = 16\): \(\xi = 2.39\). Exact: \(1/\ln 1.5 = 2.47\). At \(h = 0.5\) you get \(\xi \approx 5\times10^9\): the cat state has a second eigenvalue equal to 1.

Why must \(\xi\) be finite for any MPS with finite \(\chi\)?

\(T\) is a finite \(\chi^2\times\chi^2\) matrix, so unless \(|\lambda_2| = 1\) exactly, correlations decay exponentially.

At a critical point the true \(\xi\) is infinite, but any finite-\(\chi\) MPS has a finite one. So \(\chi\) plays the role of a system size. Two scaling laws follow (Tagliacozzo et al. arXiv:0801.2970; Pollmann et al. arXiv:0812.2903):

\(S = \frac{c}{6}\log_2\xi + \text{const}, \qquad \xi \propto \chi^{\kappa},\;\; \kappa \approx 2.03 \text{ for } c = \tfrac12\)
Your data\(h = 1\), \(\chi = 6\) to 16: \(\xi\) grows from 55 to 434 sites, and the fit of \(S\) against \(\log_2\xi\) gives \(c \approx 0.51\). Each run needed 1000 steps (2000 sites) to converge.

Why does iDMRG at criticality need so many steps?

The chain has to grow well beyond \(\xi(\chi)\), which is hundreds of sites here, before the centre forgets the edges.
