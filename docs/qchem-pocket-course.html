The question of this course: given where the atoms of a molecule sit, what is the lowest energy of its electrons? Almost everything chemists want follows from that one number computed at many geometries: the bond length is where it is lowest, the bond energy is how much it rises when you pull the atoms apart, and a reaction energy is the difference between the totals before and after.

Recipe. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so hold them fixed at positions \(\mathbf R_A\) with charges \(Z_A\) and solve for the electrons alone (the Born–Oppenheimer approximation). In hartree atomic units (\(\hbar = m_e = e = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1\)) the electrons' Hamiltonian is

\(H = -\sum_i \tfrac12\nabla_i^2 \;-\; \sum_{i,A} \frac{Z_A}{|\mathbf r_i - \mathbf R_A|} \;+\; \sum_{i<j} \frac{1}{|\mathbf r_i - \mathbf r_j|} \;+\; E_\text{nuc}\)

Kinetic energy, attraction to the nuclei, repulsion between electrons, and the constant repulsion between the fixed nuclei. The electron–electron term is what makes it hard: without it, each electron could be solved for on its own.

Energies come out in hartree (Ha); \(1\) Ha \(= 27.2\) eV. Chemists quote energy differences in kcal/mol, and \(1\) kcal/mol \(\approx 1.6\times10^{-3}\) Ha.

Your dataWater in its smallest basis: exact total energy \(E = -75.0126471190\) Ha, of which the nuclear repulsion is \(E_\text{nuc} = 9.188258\) Ha. Notebook 6.

M. Born and R. Oppenheimer, Ann. Phys. 389, 457 (1927); A. Szabo and N. S. Ostlund, Modern Quantum Chemistry, Dover (1996), chapter 2.

You want a reaction energy to 1 kcal/mol, and each molecule's total energy is about \(-75\) Ha. To what relative precision must each total be right?

\(1.6\times10^{-3}/75 \approx 2\times10^{-5}\): a few parts in \(10^5\). Chemistry lives in tiny differences of large numbers, which is why approximate methods have to be so accurate.

The electrons live in continuous space, but a computer needs a finite list. So pick \(K\) one-electron functions, orbitals \(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_K\), and allow each electron only combinations of them. \(K\) plays the role of the chain length.

Worked example: water in STO-3G. The smallest standard choice, the STO-3G basis set, takes one function per atomic orbital of each free atom, centred on that atom:

  • oxygen: 1s, 2s, 2p\(_x\), 2p\(_y\), 2p\(_z\), five functions;
  • each hydrogen: 1s, one function each.

That is \(K = 7\) orbitals for 10 electrons. Larger basis sets (such as cc-pVDZ) add more functions per atom and get closer to the continuum limit, at the price of a larger \(K\).

The answer is then exact within the basis: the best any method can do is the lowest eigenvalue of \(H\) restricted to these orbitals. Everything that follows lives inside that restriction.

In the code: gto.M(atom="O 0 0 0; H 0 0.757 0.587; H 0 -0.757 0.587", basis="sto-3g") (positions in ångström), PySCF.

Electrons are fermions: swapping two of them must flip the sign of the wavefunction. A simple product "electron 1 in \(\chi_1\), electron 2 in \(\chi_2\)" does not do that, so it is not allowed.

Here \(\chi\) is a spin-orbital, an orbital together with a spin, \(\phi_p\uparrow\) or \(\phi_p\downarrow\); \(K\) orbitals give \(2K\) spin-orbitals.

Two electrons. Antisymmetrize the product by hand:

\(\Phi(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\chi_1(x_1)\chi_2(x_2) - \chi_2(x_1)\chi_1(x_2)\big] = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\det\begin{pmatrix} \chi_1(x_1) & \chi_2(x_1) \\ \chi_1(x_2) & \chi_2(x_2) \end{pmatrix}\)

Swapping \(x_1 \leftrightarrow x_2\) swaps the rows, and a determinant changes sign when two rows swap. If \(\chi_1 = \chi_2\), two columns are equal and \(\Phi = 0\): no two electrons in the same spin-orbital, which is the Pauli principle.

\(N\) electrons. The same construction with an \(N\times N\) matrix \(\chi_j(x_i)\) is a Slater determinant. It is the fermion version of a product state: each electron in its own spin-orbital, and nothing more. A Slater determinant is fixed entirely by which spin-orbitals are filled.

Szabo and Ostlund, chapter 2.

Water has 10 electrons in 7 orbitals. In a single Slater determinant, how many orbitals hold two electrons, if all are paired?

Five, each with one \(\uparrow\) and one \(\downarrow\) electron; the other two orbitals are empty.

Since a determinant is fixed by which spin-orbitals are filled, write it as a list of occupations, one entry per orbital: 0 (empty), \(\uparrow\), \(\downarrow\) or 2 (both). This is the language of DMRG: a basis state of a chain.

Worked example. The Hartree–Fock determinant of water (next part) fills the five lowest orbitals:

\(|\Phi_0\rangle = |2\,2\,2\,2\,2\,0\,0\rangle\)

To make this precise, let \(a^\dagger_{p\sigma}\) create an electron in orbital \(p\) with spin \(\sigma\). Then a determinant is a product of creation operators on the empty state, \(|\Phi\rangle = a^\dagger_{1\uparrow}a^\dagger_{1\downarrow}a^\dagger_{2\uparrow}\cdots|0\rangle\). Antisymmetry is now an operator rule:

\(a^\dagger_{p\sigma}a^\dagger_{q\tau} = -\,a^\dagger_{q\tau}a^\dagger_{p\sigma}\)

so the order in which you create electrons fixes the sign of the state, and \(a^\dagger_{p\sigma}a^\dagger_{p\sigma} = 0\) is Pauli again. This is second quantization: the wavefunction's antisymmetry has moved into the operators.

Szabo and Ostlund, chapter 2; T. Helgaker, P. Jørgensen and J. Olsen, Molecular Electronic-Structure Theory, Wiley (2000).

What is \(a^\dagger_{2\uparrow}a^\dagger_{1\uparrow}|0\rangle\) in terms of \(a^\dagger_{1\uparrow}a^\dagger_{2\uparrow}|0\rangle\)?

Its negative. Same occupations, opposite sign: the sign depends on an agreed order, and a chain code fixes that order along the chain.

The first approximation every calculation makes: find the Slater determinant with the lowest energy. That is Hartree–Fock (HF), the mean-field theory of chemistry, like a product-state ansatz for a spin chain.

Recipe (the self-consistent field):

  • Guess the orbitals and fill the lowest ones.
  • Each electron feels the nuclei plus the average repulsion of all the others. Build that one-electron Hamiltonian (the Fock matrix) and diagonalize it: new orbitals.
  • Refill the lowest, rebuild the average, repeat until the orbitals stop changing.

The result is a set of \(K\) orbitals ordered by energy: the lowest \(N/2\) doubly occupied, the rest empty. These HF orbitals are the \(\phi_p\) the rest of the course uses, and the HF determinant \(|\Phi_0\rangle\) is the reference state.

Because \(E_\text{HF} = \langle\Phi_0|H|\Phi_0\rangle\) is an expectation value, it is an upper bound on the exact energy in the basis.

In the code: mf = scf.RHF(mol).run(); mf.e_tot is \(E_\text{HF}\), mf.mo_coeff the orbitals.

Szabo and Ostlund, chapter 3.

In the DMRG language, what is the bond dimension of a single Slater determinant written in its own orbitals?

1. Each orbital is surely empty, singly or doubly occupied, so the state is a product of site states: no entanglement between orbitals.

Everything Hartree–Fock misses is called the correlation energy:

\(E_\text{corr} = E_\text{exact} - E_\text{HF}\)

It is what electrons gain by avoiding each other instantaneously, rather than only on average. In a mean field, each electron sees a smeared-out cloud of the others; in reality, where one electron is, the others are less likely to be.

Your dataWater, STO-3G: \(E_\text{corr} = -0.0496\) Ha, under 0.1% of the total energy. Notebook 6.

Small, but it decides chemistry: \(0.05\) Ha is about 30 kcal/mol, the size of a reaction energy. A method that drops it gets reaction energies wrong by as much as the reaction energy itself.

All of quantum chemistry beyond HF is about recovering \(E_\text{corr}\). The exact answer within the basis has a name, FCI (Part 3), and DMRG is one way to get it.

Can \(E_\text{corr}\) be positive?

No. \(E_\text{HF}\) is the expectation value in one state of the basis, so it is at least the lowest eigenvalue: \(E_\text{corr} \le 0\).

To put a molecule into DMRG, write \(H\) with the operators \(a^\dagger, a\) of the HF orbitals, as you would a lattice model:

\(H = E_\text{core} + \sum_{pq,\sigma} h_{pq}\, a^\dagger_{p\sigma} a_{q\sigma} + \frac12 \sum_{pqrs,\sigma\tau} g_{pqrs}\, a^\dagger_{p\sigma} a^\dagger_{r\tau} a_{s\tau} a_{q\sigma}\)

Read it term by term:

  • \(h_{pq}\) moves one electron from orbital \(q\) to \(p\), keeping its spin: a hopping, with the kinetic energy and the attraction to the nuclei inside.
  • \(g_{pqrs}\) is the Coulomb repulsion between two electrons, one moving \(q \to p\) and the other \(s \to r\). It is the integral \(\int \phi_p\phi_q(1)\,\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}\,\phi_r\phi_s(2)\), written \((pq|rs)\) by chemists.
  • \(E_\text{core}\) is a constant: the repulsion of the nuclei (plus, later, frozen core electrons).

Compare the TFIM. There, each site talks to its neighbour. Here every pair of orbitals hops and every quadruple interacts: a fermion chain with all-to-all couplings.

Helgaker, Jørgensen and Olsen, Wiley (2000).

Which \(g\) is the plain repulsion between an electron sitting in orbital \(p\) and one sitting in orbital \(r\) (neither moves)?

\(g_{pprr} = (pp|rr)\): set \(q = p\) and \(s = r\), and the term becomes \(g_{pprr}\,n_{p\sigma} n_{r\tau}\) for different spin-orbitals, a density–density repulsion.

Once the orbitals are chosen, the molecule is gone: all that is left are two arrays of numbers and a constant. PySCF computes them; DMRG never sees anything else.

Check the meaning. Put \(|\Phi_0\rangle\), the HF determinant, into \(H\). With \(i, j\) running over the doubly occupied orbitals,

\(E_\text{HF} = E_\text{core} + 2\sum_i h_{ii} + \sum_{ij}\big[2g_{iijj} - g_{ijji}\big]\)

The \(2h_{ii}\): two electrons per orbital, each with its one-electron energy. The \(2g_{iijj}\): the plain (direct) Coulomb repulsion between the four electron pairs of orbitals \(i\) and \(j\). The \(-g_{ijji}\): exchange, a correction only between same-spin electrons, coming from the antisymmetry.

The exact answer within a basis is called full configuration interaction (FCI). It is exact diagonalization: write \(H\) in the basis of every Slater determinant and find the lowest eigenvalue.

Count the determinants. \(H\) conserves the number of \(\uparrow\) and \(\downarrow\) electrons, so choose which \(N_\uparrow\) of the \(K\) orbitals hold an \(\uparrow\) electron, and independently which \(N_\downarrow\) hold a \(\downarrow\):

\(\dim = \binom{K}{N_\uparrow}\binom{K}{N_\downarrow}\)

Water: \(K = 7\), \(N_\uparrow = N_\downarrow = 5\), so \(\binom75^2 = 21^2 = 441\).

Your dataFCI dimension: H₂O, STO-3G (\(K = 7\)): \(4.4\times10^2\). N₂, STO-3G (\(K = 10\)): \(1.4\times10^4\). N₂, cc-pVDZ (\(K = 28\)): \(1.4\times10^{12}\). Benzene, cc-pVDZ (\(K = 114\)): \(1.9\times10^{45}\). Notebook 6.

This is the same wall as for spin chains: each orbital you add multiplies the space. A realistic basis set is out of reach of FCI even for a two-atom molecule.

In the code: fci.FCI(mf).kernel(), PySCF.

Check the N₂ STO-3G number: 14 electrons, 10 orbitals.

\(N_\uparrow = N_\downarrow = 7\), \(\binom{10}{7} = 120\), and \(120^2 = 14\,400 \approx 1.4\times10^4\).

The usual way round the wall: solve exactly only where it matters. Split the orbitals in three:

  • core: the lowest orbitals (such as the 1s of each heavy atom), kept doubly occupied;
  • active: a window of orbitals around the highest occupied ones, treated exactly;
  • virtual: the highest orbitals, kept empty.

The active window has its own \(h\) and \(g\), and the frozen core electrons move into the constant \(E_\text{core}\). FCI on the window is called CASCI, and the window is written (electrons, orbitals).

Worked example. A (6, 6) active space: 6 electrons in 6 orbitals, \(N_\uparrow = N_\downarrow = 3\), so \(\binom63^2 = 400\) determinants, however large the full basis.

The price: everything outside the window is treated as in HF, so the answer depends on choosing the window well. DMRG's job in chemistry is to replace FCI when the active space is too big for it, and, as Part 5 shows, to help choose the window.

In the code: itg.get_rhf_integrals(mf, ncore=..., ncas=...) returns the Hamiltonian of the window; pyscf.mcscf.CASCI solves it with FCI.

\(14 - 4 = 10\). Each frozen core orbital takes 2 electrons.

Between HF and FCI sits the method chemists use by default: coupled cluster. It starts from the HF determinant and adds excitations.

Machinery. A double excitation \(a^\dagger_a a^\dagger_b a_j a_i\) moves two electrons from occupied orbitals \(i, j\) to empty orbitals \(a, b\). Collect all of them, with unknown amplitudes \(t\), into one operator \(T_2\), and singles into \(T_1\). The ansatz is

\(|\psi_\text{CC}\rangle = e^{T_1 + T_2}\,|\Phi_0\rangle\)

That is CCSD. Expanding the exponential also produces products such as \(\tfrac12T_2^2\), four-electron excitations whose amplitudes come for free from the doubles. CCSD(T) adds triple excitations as a perturbative correction. The cost grows only polynomially with \(K\), not exponentially.

The amplitudes are found by projecting the Schrödinger equation, not by minimizing \(\langle H\rangle\), so the energy is not variational: nothing keeps it above the exact one.

Why should coupled cluster ever fail? Pull the two atoms of H₂ apart and watch the HF determinant stop being a good start.

Set-up. Smallest basis: one 1s function on each atom, \(a\) and \(b\). Far apart they barely overlap, and the HF orbitals are the bonding and antibonding combinations

\(\sigma_g = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(a + b), \qquad \sigma_u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(a - b)\)

HF puts both electrons in \(\sigma_g\). Expand \(a^\dagger_{g\uparrow}a^\dagger_{g\downarrow}\) in atomic operators:

\(|\sigma_g^2\rangle = \tfrac12\big(\underbrace{a^\dagger_{a\uparrow}a^\dagger_{a\downarrow} + a^\dagger_{b\uparrow}a^\dagger_{b\downarrow}}_{\text{both on one atom}} + \underbrace{a^\dagger_{a\uparrow}a^\dagger_{b\downarrow} + a^\dagger_{b\uparrow}a^\dagger_{a\downarrow}}_{\text{one on each}}\big)|0\rangle\)

Half the weight is H\(^+\) + H\(^-\), costly at large distance, so HF's energy is far too high.

The exact state of two neutral atoms is the "one on each" part alone. The doubly excited determinant \(|\sigma_u^2\rangle\) has the same four terms with the last two signs flipped, so

\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\sigma_g^2\rangle - |\sigma_u^2\rangle\big) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(a^\dagger_{a\uparrow}a^\dagger_{b\downarrow} + a^\dagger_{b\uparrow}a^\dagger_{a\downarrow}\big)|0\rangle\)

Two determinants with equal weight: the HF determinant carries only \(1/2\) of the exact state. No expansion around it can be a small correction.

Szabo and Ostlund (minimal-basis H₂, chapters 2–4).

How can CCSD(T) give an energy below the exact one, when HF never can?

Coupled cluster is not variational: its energy is not an expectation value of \(H\) in a state, so the upper bound does not apply.

N₂ shows two kinds of correlation energy, and chemists name them differently.

  • Dynamic: HF is a good start, and the correlation is many small corrections from electrons dodging each other. N₂ at 1.1 Å: the HF determinant carries 0.917 of the exact state. Coupled cluster is built for this.
  • Static (or strong): a few determinants with large weights, as in stretched H₂ (two at weight 1/2) or N₂ at 2.8 Å, where the HF determinant carries only 0.078. Methods that expand around one determinant fail.

What DMRG cares about. Neither: it does not expand around any determinant. Its cost is set, as for spin chains, by the entanglement across each cut of the orbital chain. Static correlation in a handful of orbitals is a few strongly entangled sites, which DMRG handles well. That is why DMRG is mostly run on an active space of strongly correlated orbitals, with the dynamic correlation of the remaining orbitals added by cheaper methods (see the review below).

How do you hand the molecular \(H\) to a DMRG code? The same way as any lattice model: as a sum of operator strings with coefficients, which the package compresses into an MPO.

The letters. block2 writes an operator string as letters: c and d create and annihilate a spin-up electron, C and D a spin-down one. add_sum_term(string, array) adds \(\sum\) array[i, j, ...] times the string, with the indices in the order of the letters.

def chem_mpo(h1e, g2e, ecore):
    b = driver.expr_builder()
    b.add_const(ecore)
    for s in ["cd", "CD"]:
        b.add_sum_term(s, h1e)
    g = 0.5 * g2e.transpose(0, 2, 3, 1)
    for s in ["ccdd", "cCDd",
              "CcdD", "CCDD"]:
        b.add_sum_term(s, g)
    expr = b.finalize(adjust_order=True,
                      fermionic_ops="cdCD")
    return driver.get_mpo(expr)

Worked example. "cd" with h1e is \(\sum_{pq} h_{pq}\, a^\dagger_{p\uparrow} a_{q\uparrow}\), the spin-up hopping. For the two-electron term take \(\sigma = \uparrow\), \(\tau = \downarrow\): \(a^\dagger_{p\uparrow} a^\dagger_{r\downarrow} a_{s\downarrow} a_{q\uparrow}\) is the string "cCDd" with indices in the order \(p, r, s, q\). Its coefficient is \(\tfrac12 g_{pqrs}\), and transpose(0, 2, 3, 1) makes an array \(T\) with \(T_{prsq} = g_{pqrs}\), indices in the operator order. The four strings are the four spin choices \(\sigma\tau\).

finalize sorts every string into site order, with the fermion signs of the letters in fermionic_ops (the Jordan–Wigner string), and get_mpo compresses the sum into an MPO.

Which string, on one site \(i\), gives the Hubbard interaction \(U n_{i\uparrow} n_{i\downarrow}\)?

"cdCD" with all four indices equal to \(i\): \(a^\dagger_{i\uparrow}a_{i\uparrow}\,a^\dagger_{i\downarrow}a_{i\downarrow} = n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}\). Notebook 6's last exercise builds the Hubbard chain this way.

The TFIM MPO has \(D = 3\) because each bond only has to remember "nothing yet", "an \(X\) waiting for its partner" or "done". Ask the same question for one term \(a^\dagger_p a^\dagger_r a_s a_q\) of the molecular \(H\), with a cut somewhere in the chain:

Count what the bond must remember, with \(\ell\) orbitals left of the cut:

  • one of the four operators on the left: which one and where, about \(\ell\) channels;
  • two on the left: which pair, about \(\ell^2\) channels;
  • three on the left: which triple, \(\ell^3\), too many. The trick: sum the triple with its integrals \(g\) on the left into one operator per remaining right index (a complementary operator), so about \(K\) channels again.

The pairs dominate, so \(D\) grows as \(K^2\), however the MPO is built.

DMRG is variational, like HF, so it cannot land below the exact energy by more than numerical noise. And it never assumed one determinant was dominant: at 2.8 Å the HF determinant carries 0.078 of the state, and DMRG does not notice.

The point is not N₂ in STO-3G, where FCI is cheap. It is that DMRG's cost does not grow with the number of important determinants, only with the entanglement. So it keeps working when \(K\) is too big for FCI, as long as the entanglement stays moderate.

(Lesson 2) Why does DMRG's answer depend on the basis set even when it matches FCI to \(10^{-11}\)?

FCI is exact only within the basis. A small basis like STO-3G is far from the continuum limit, whatever the solver.

On a lattice, the chain order is given. For a molecule you choose it, and the choice changes the entanglement of every cut. Notebook 6 tests this on H₁₀, ten hydrogen atoms in a line 1.5 Å apart (10 orbitals, FCI still instant).

Two choices of orbitals, which give the same FCI energy:

  • HF orbitals, ordered by energy. Each is spread over the whole chain.
  • Localized orbitals: rotate the occupied orbitals among themselves, and the empty ones among themselves, to make each as compact as possible (Boys localization); then order them by position along the molecule. In this basis \(h_{pq}\) is nearly tridiagonal: a tight-binding chain.

The Coulomb integrals still decay only as one over distance in both bases, so the MPO stays big. What changes is the entanglement of the state across each cut.

Why does the HF basis need so much more \(\chi\), when it describes the same state?

Each HF orbital spreads over all atoms, so every cut of the orbital chain separates electrons that are correlated in space: high entanglement. Localized orbitals ordered along the molecule make each cut a cut in space, as in a gapped 1D chain.

Most molecules are not lines, so there is no obvious order. A standard recipe puts strongly coupled orbitals next to each other.

Recipe. Build a graph: one node per orbital, edge weights \(w_{pq}\) measuring how strongly orbitals \(p\) and \(q\) are coupled. Form its graph Laplacian \(L = \text{diag}(\sum_q w_{pq}) - w\). Its lowest eigenvector is constant; order the orbitals by the entries of the second one, the Fiedler vector.

Worked example. Three orbitals, 1–2 and 2–3 coupled with weight 1, 1–3 not at all:

\(L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = 1\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\)

The eigenvalues are 0, 1, 3, so \((1, 0, -1)\) is the Fiedler vector, and sorting by it gives the order 1, 2, 3: the coupled pairs end up neighbours.

Fiedler returned the chain backwards. Why is that just as good?

Reversing a chain gives the same set of cuts, so the same entanglement and the same DMRG cost. (An eigenvector is fixed only up to sign, and flipping the sign reverses the order.)

For a real molecule there is no FCI to compare with. The course's rule takes its place: the energy error tracks the discarded weight \(\varepsilon\), so run several \(\chi\), fit \(E\) against \(\varepsilon\), and read off \(E\) at \(\varepsilon = 0\).

Which orbitals carry the correlation? DMRG answers directly, by measuring each site's entanglement with the rest.

Recipe. Take the reduced density matrix \(\rho_p\) of one orbital, a \(4\times4\) matrix since \(d = 4\). Because the state has fixed \(N\) and \(S_z\), \(\rho_p\) is diagonal in the four site states, with the probabilities \(w_0, w_\uparrow, w_\downarrow, w_{\uparrow\downarrow}\) of finding the orbital empty, singly or doubly occupied. The single-orbital entropy is

\(s_p = -\sum_{n} w_n \log_2 w_n\)

It is 0 for an orbital that is surely empty or surely full, as in HF, and at most 2 bits, when all four probabilities are \(1/4\).

Worked example. Stretched H₂ ("Breaking H₂ by hand"): orbital \(\sigma_g\) is empty with probability 1/2 and doubly occupied with probability 1/2, so \(s_{\sigma_g} = 1\) bit. A strongly correlated orbital has a large \(s_p\).

In the code: driver.get_orbital_entropies(ket, orb_type=1), in nats; divide by \(\ln 2\) for bits.

J. Rissler, R. M. Noack and S. R. White, arXiv:cond-mat/0508524; Chem. Phys. 323, 519 (2006).

In stretched H₂, use the atomic orbitals \(a, b\) instead. What is \(s_a\)?

1 bit as well. In \((a^\dagger_{a\uparrow}a^\dagger_{b\downarrow} + a^\dagger_{b\uparrow}a^\dagger_{a\downarrow})|0\rangle/\sqrt2\), orbital \(a\) holds one \(\uparrow\) or one \(\downarrow\) electron, probability 1/2 each.

The entropies turn the choice of active space ("Active spaces") from intuition into a measurement: the orbitals with large \(s_p\) must be treated exactly; those with \(s_p \approx 0\) can be frozen or dropped.

Orbitals 4 to 9 form the active space, and 0 to 3 are doubly occupied. Why 6 active electrons, out of 14?

Orbitals 0 to 3 hold \(4 \times 2 = 8\) electrons, leaving \(14 - 8 = 6\): the six electrons of the triple bond.

You now know what the electronic structure problem is, why HF and coupled cluster are not enough when bonds break, and how a molecule becomes a DMRG chain. What a production code such as block2 adds:

  • Any Hamiltonian. expr_builder takes any sum of fermion or spin operator strings ("Writing H term by term").
  • Spin symmetry. SymmetryTypes.SU2 works with total-spin multiplets instead of \(S_z\) states, 3 per site instead of 4, and fixes the total spin \(S\). Point-group labels shrink the blocks further.
  • Large active spaces, up to about a hundred orbitals, with parallel and distributed sweeps (Zhai et al., "DMRG on water").
  • More than the energy: excited states, density matrices, orbital entropies and dynamical correlation functions from the same MPS.

DMRG and quantum computers. DMRG on large active spaces is the classical reference that quantum algorithms for chemistry are measured against. Lee et al. weigh quantum phase estimation against classical methods such as DMRG for ground-state energies, and conclude that evidence for an exponential quantum advantage across chemistry has yet to be found.

(Lesson 7) Why does the correlation energy matter for chemistry, when it is under 0.1% of the total?

Chemistry is energy differences, and \(E_\text{corr}\) (about 30 kcal/mol for water) is as large as a reaction energy.

(Lesson 26) Without FCI, how do you give an error bar on a DMRG energy?

Run several \(\chi\), extrapolate \(E\) linearly in the discarded weight to zero, and use the spread between different fits as the uncertainty.
